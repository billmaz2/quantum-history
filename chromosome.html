Chromosome Structure & Centromere Proteins

A decade-by-decade survey of the top papers defining the one-chromatid–one-DNA model, nucleosome architecture, and the centromeric protein machinery · 1900–2026
Abstract. From 1900 to 2026, the understanding of chromosome structure has advanced from cytological description to atomic-resolution structural biology. The central framework: a single chromatid is a continuous DNA double helix folded into nucleosome arrays; centromere identity is epigenetically defined by the histone H3 variant CENP-A, which nucleates the CCAN (constitutive centromere-associated network) and recruits outer kinetochore proteins for microtubule attachment. This paper traces the defining publications by decade, highlighting how each layer of molecular organization was uncovered and how the centromeric proteins interact to ensure faithful chromosome segregation. All key papers are linked via DOI or PubMed.

1. Establishment of the Chromosomal Theory of Heredity (1902–1910)

Foundational Cytology

Sutton (1902, 1903) — Walter Sutton published a series of papers explicitly linking Mendelian “factors” to the physical entities of chromosomes. He observed that chromosomes occur in pairs, segregate during meiosis, and independently assort — exactly paralleling Mendelian inheritance. DOI: 10.2307/1535510 JSTOR: 1535510

Boveri (1902) — Theodor Boveri used sea urchin fertilization experiments to demonstrate that the nucleus contains the information determining individual development. His work complemented Sutton's, leading to the Chromosomal Theory of Heredity. URN: nbn:de:hebis:30-1036865

Stevens (1905); Wilson (1905) — Nettie Stevens and Edmund Beecher Wilson independently discovered sex chromosomes, linking a specific trait (sex) to a specific chromosome for the first time. PubMed: 17748139 DOI: 10.1126/science.22.564.500

Morgan (1910) — Thomas Hunt Morgan discovered the white-eye mutation in Drosophila and demonstrated its X-linked inheritance, providing the first definitive proof that genes reside on chromosomes. DOI: 10.1126/science.32.812.120

Contribution to the “one-chromatid–one-DNA” model: This era established chromosomes as the physical carriers of heredity. DNA had not yet been identified as the genetic material, and the concept of a chromatid containing a single DNA molecule did not exist.

2. The One-Chromatid–One-DNA Model Solidifies (1945–1975)

Model defined Unineme

This period is the heart of the story. It bridges the discovery of DNA as the genetic material (1944) with the biochemical proof that a chromatid contains a single continuous DNA double helix (the unineme model), culminating in the nucleosome model of chromatin (1974).

The Chemical Nature of the Gene (1944–1952)

Avery, MacLeod & McCarty (1944) — Using pneumococcal transformation, Oswald Avery, Colin MacLeod, and Maclyn McCarty demonstrated that DNA — not protein — is the substance responsible for heredity. This was the first definitive evidence that the gene is chemically DNA. PubMed: 19871359 DOI: 10.1084/jem.79.2.137

Hershey & Chase (1952) — Alfred Hershey and Martha Chase used radioactive isotopes to label DNA and protein separately in bacteriophage T2, confirming that DNA enters the host cell and carries genetic information. This convinced the remaining skeptics. PubMed: 12981234 DOI: 10.1085/jgp.36.1.39

Double Helix Structure (1953)

Watson & Crick (1953) — James Watson and Francis Crick proposed the double-helix structure of DNA, providing the molecular basis for information storage and replication. This immediately suggested a copying mechanism: each strand serves as a template for a new complementary strand. PubMed: 13054692 DOI: 10.1038/171737a0

Proof of Semiconservative Replication (1958)

Meselson & Stahl (1958) — Matthew Meselson and Franklin Stahl used density-gradient centrifugation to prove that DNA replicates semiconservatively, exactly as Watson and Crick predicted. This established that each daughter DNA molecule contains one old strand and one new strand. PubMed: 16590258 DOI: 10.1073/pnas.44.7.671

The Unineme Model: One Chromatid = One DNA Double Helix

The concept that each chromatid contains a single continuous DNA double helix (the unineme model) was confirmed by several lines of evidence in the 1950s–1970s. Key experiments included:

  • Taylor (1957) — Autoradiographic demonstration that tritiated thymidine incorporates into chromosomes during one round of DNA synthesis, appearing in both chromatids at the first division, but in only one chromatid after a second round without label.
  • Gall (1963) — Kinetic studies of DNase digestion of lampbrush chromosomes supported the view that each chromatid contains only two polynucleotide chains.
  • Huberman & Riggs (1968) — DNA fiber autoradiography showed replication proceeds bidirectionally from tandem initiation points, consistent with a single continuous DNA molecule per chromatid.

By the early 1970s, the unineme model was widely accepted. The summary statement in modern reviews: “It is now generally accepted that there is only a single DNA molecule running throughout the length of a chromatid—that is, the undivided chromosome is unineme.” DOI: 10.1016/S1067-5701(98)80022-3 Callan Review (1972) DOI: 10.1098/rspb.1972.0041

Chromatin Repeating Unit: The Nucleosome (1974)

Kornberg (1974) — Roger Kornberg proposed that the basic repeating unit of chromatin consists of two copies each of four core histones (H2A, H2B, H3, H4) associated with ~200 bp of DNA. This provided the first molecular model of how DNA is packaged within the chromatid. PubMed: 4825889 DOI: 10.1126/science.184.4139.868

The 1945–1975 Synthesis: DNA is the genetic material (1944, 1952); its structure is a double helix (1953); it replicates semiconservatively (1958); each chromatid contains one continuous DNA double helix (unineme model); and that DNA is packaged with histones into repeating nucleosome units (1974). This is the complete foundation of the “one chromosome, one chromatid, one DNA molecule” model.

3. Nucleosome Structure and the Transition to Centromere Biology (1975–1997)

Structural biology Chromatin

Luger et al. (1997) — Karolin Luger and colleagues solved the 2.8 Å crystal structure of the nucleosome core particle, revealing the precise geometry of the histone octamer (one (H3-H4)₂ tetramer + two H2A-H2B dimers) with DNA wrapped around it. This remains the foundational structural image of chromatin. PubMed: 9305837 DOI: 10.1038/38444

4. Discovery of Centromere Proteins and CENP-A (1980–1990s)

Centromere Autoantigens

Palmer et al. (1985, 1987, 1991) — Using autoantibodies from CREST syndrome patients, researchers identified CENP-A, CENP-B, and CENP-C. In 1985, Palmer and Margolis showed that CENP-A is a nucleosome component. In 1987, a 17-kD centromere protein (CENP-A) was shown to copurify with nucleosome core particles. In 1991, Palmer and colleagues purified CENP-A and demonstrated it is a histone H3 variant — the experimental origin of the “epigenetic mark” concept for centromeres. PubMed: 2580229 (1985) PubMed: 3558482 (1987) PubMed: 2023923 (1991)

Earnshaw et al. (1987) — William Earnshaw and colleagues characterized the centromere protein antigens using CREST sera, establishing CENP-A, CENP-B, and CENP-C as distinct centromeric components. PubMed: 2435739 DOI: 10.1083/jcb.104.4.817

5. CCAN Assembly and CENP-A Nucleosome Structure (2000–2015)

Structural biology Epigenetics

Black et al. (2004) — Barbara Black and colleagues identified the CATD (CENP-A Targeting Domain) within the histone-fold region of CENP-A, required for centromere targeting. This linked CENP-A's unique structural features directly to its centromeric localization. PubMed: 15282608 DOI: 10.1038/nature02766

Tachiwana et al. (2011); Kato et al. (2011); Screpanti et al. (2011) — Multiple groups reconstituted human CENP-A nucleosomes and solved crystal structures. The (CENP-A/H4)₂ heterotetramer shows a 9–14° rotational offset relative to (H3/H4)₂, leading to a more compact overall conformation. Key residues His104 and Leu112 stabilize this rotated conformation; the H104G+L112C double mutation abolishes centromere targeting. PubMed: 21743476 (Tachiwana 2011) DOI: 10.1038/nature10258 PubMed: 22052973 (Kato 2011) DOI: 10.1126/science.1213492

CCAN composition and hierarchy — The CCAN is a group of constitutive centromere-associated proteins that provide a structural core for recruiting outer kinetochore and inner centromere proteins. The CENP-A nucleosome serves as the epigenetic platform for CCAN assembly, initiating hierarchical recruitment: CENP-A/CENP-C → CCAN → KMN network (KNL1–Mis12–Ndc80) → microtubules.

6. CENP-C as Structural Hub and Kinetochore–Microtubule Attachment (2011–2024)

Kinetochore Cryo-EM

Petrovic et al. (2011); Screpanti et al. (2011) — Petrovic and colleagues solved the direct interaction of CENP-C with the Mis12 complex (Mis12C), showing that the N-terminal fragment of CENP-C binds Mis12C at a 1:1 stoichiometry. This binding is compatible with Mis12C simultaneously engaging Ndc80C and KNL1. EM analysis revealed that CENP-C binding converts Mis12C from a flexible, bent conformation to a rigid, straight rod — providing a conformational basis for ordered KMN assembly. PubMed: 22014572 (Petrovic 2011) DOI: 10.1083/jcb.201107079

Yu et al. (2024) — Published in Nature Structural & Molecular Biology, this study presented the high-resolution cryo-EM structure of the human KMN network, revealing extensive rigid interfaces between Mis12C, Ndc80C, and KNL1C. Mis12C exists in an autoinhibited conformation in the unphosphorylated state; Aurora B phosphorylation of Dsn1 Ser100 and Ser109 relieves this autoinhibition, restricting outer kinetochore assembly to functional centromeres. PubMed: 38366227 DOI: 10.1038/s41594-024-01231-8

7. Three-Dimensional Centromere Architecture and CENP-A Chaperones (2026)

Cutting edge 3D organization

Science Advances (2026) — A landmark study revealed the three-dimensional hierarchical architecture of centromeres during interphase. Key findings:

  • Shell structure: HJURP (the CENP-A-specific chaperone) and the Mis18 complex occupy a chromatin-poor central cavity, surrounded by a concentric ring of CENP-A nucleosomes and CCAN.
  • Directed spanning by CENP-C: CENP-C spans the assembly with a defined N-to-C orientation, connecting the chaperone core to CENP-A chromatin and CCAN.
  • CENP-C regulates architecture via HJURP: CENP-C knockdown reduces centromeric HJURP localization by 63% and disrupts the ring structure; HJURP knockdown does not affect CENP-C localization.
  • Cell-cycle-dependent assembly–disassembly: PLK1-driven interphase assembly forms the shell; CDK-driven entry into mitosis expels HJURP, collapses the shell into a small dot, and creates the site for outer kinetochore assembly.

8. Summary of Decade-by-Decade Evolution

Decade Core Conceptual Leap Key Molecular Entities
1900–1910 Chromosomes are the physical carriers of heredity Chromosomes, genes
1944–1953 DNA is the genetic material; double helix structure DNA double helix
1958–1974 Semiconservative replication; unineme model; nucleosome repeating unit Unineme chromatid, nucleosome
1985–1991 Centromeres contain a specific histone variant, CENP-A CENP-A, CENP-B, CENP-C
2004–2011 CENP-A nucleosome structure solved; CATD identified CENP-A/H4 tetramer
2011–2024 CENP-C links CCAN to KMN; KMN high-resolution structure CCAN, Mis12C, Ndc80C
2026 3D centromere shell architecture and cell-cycle dynamics HJURP, Mis18, CENP-C directed spanning

Core logic (1900–2026): The physical carrier of heredity was progressively refined from “chromosome” to “a specific nucleosome type (CENP-A) on a specific DNA sequence.” Centromere function depends on a hierarchical protein recruitment cascade — each layer serves as the structural platform for the next, with precise stoichiometry and conformational regulation ensuring the fidelity of chromosome segregation.
