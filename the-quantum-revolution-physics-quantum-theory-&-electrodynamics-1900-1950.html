The Quantum Revolution, 1900–1950

A conceptual and experimental timeline of quantum theory and electrodynamics — from Planck's "act of desperation" in 1900 to the renormalized quantum electrodynamics of Schwinger, Tomonaga, and Feynman in 1948–50.

Each entry explains the old physical model, the problem it could not solve, the new model that replaced it, and the key equations — alongside the experimental discoveries that forced the revolution.

The world physics inhabited in 1900

By 1900, classical physics seemed almost complete. Newton's mechanics governed particles, Maxwell's equations governed light and fields, and thermodynamics tied them together. Lord Kelvin famously remarked that only "two small clouds" remained on the horizon: the failure to detect the ether (which became relativity) and the blackbody spectrum (which became quantum theory). Within fifty years, both clouds had grown into complete reconstructions of physics — with the electron, the photon, the nucleus, the wavefunction, spin, antimatter, and the quantum field emerging along the way.

11900–1901 · Berlin — the quantum of energy is born

Planck's Quantum of Action

Old idea
Classical electrodynamics: hot matter emits light continuously, and the energy of a vibrating atom (an oscillator) can take any value. From equipartition, every frequency of the electromagnetic field should share energy equally.
The problem — the "ultraviolet catastrophe"
The classical Rayleigh–Jeans law predicts infinite energy at high frequencies. Worse, the measured spectrum of thermal radiation (black-body radiation) simply did not match any classical formula: the measured peak shifts with temperature (Wien's displacement law), a fact no classical oscillator model could reproduce.
New model
On 14 December 1900, Max Planck proposed — as "an act of desperation" — that oscillators can only emit or absorb energy in discrete packets: E = hν, where ν is frequency and h a new constant of nature. Energy is quantized. It was not yet a statement about light itself, only about the matter that emits it.
ρ(ν, T) = (8πhν³/c³) · 1/(ehν/kT − 1)Planck's radiation law (1901). Fits the entire black-body spectrum; h ≈ 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J·s. The classical limit h→0 gives back Rayleigh–Jeans — the first glimpse of a "correspondence" between quantum and classical worlds.
Key papers
  • Planck M. On the Law of Distribution of Energy in the Normal Spectrum. Annalen der Physik 4, 553–562 (1901). — English translation (PDF, free) · Alternative translation (PDF, free)
21905 · Bern — Einstein's "miracle year": the photon and relativity

The Light Quantum (Photon) & Special Relativity

Old idea
Light is a continuous electromagnetic wave in the ether (Maxwell). Waves cannot explain why metals eject electrons instantly below a threshold frequency regardless of intensity. Meanwhile, Maxwell's equations require a medium (ether) — yet the Earth's motion through it could never be detected (Michelson–Morley, 1887).
The problem
The photoelectric effect (Hertz 1887; Lenard 1902): electrons are emitted only above a threshold frequency, with kinetic energy proportional to frequency — impossible for waves. Planck's quanta were also unexplained: what physical object carries them?
New model
Einstein went further than Planck: light itself travels as particles (quanta), each with E = hν. He simultaneously abolished the ether with special relativity: space and time unify into a 4-dimensional geometry; electric and magnetic fields mix under boosts — electrodynamics was already relativistic, Newton's mechanics was not. In his third 1905 paper, Brownian motion showed atoms are real.
Emax = hν − WPhotoelectric equation (1905): kinetic energy of ejected electrons is linear in frequency; W is the metal's work function. Nobel Prize 1921.
E = mc²   ·   γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²)Mass–energy equivalence and the Lorentz factor. Electric and magnetic fields transform into each other — the first unification step toward modern electrodynamics.
Key papers
  • Einstein A. Über einen die Erzeugung und Verwandlung des Lichtes betreffenden heuristischen Gesichtspunkt. Annalen der Physik 17, 132 (1905). — Wikisource translation (free)
  • Einstein A. On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies. Annalen der Physik 17 (1905). — Wikisource translation (free)
31911–1913 · Manchester & Copenhagen — the nuclear atom

Rutherford's Nucleus & Bohr's Quantized Orbits

Old idea
J.J. Thomson's "plum pudding" model: a diffuse sphere of positive charge with electrons embedded like raisins. A planetary model was ruled out — an orbiting electron radiates and should spiral into the nucleus in ~10⁻¹⁰ seconds.
The problem
Rutherford's alpha-particle scattering (Geiger–Marsden, 1909–1911): about 1 in 8000 alpha particles bounced backward — impossible unless the positive charge is concentrated in a tiny, dense nucleus. But then, by classical electrodynamics, atoms should collapse, and every atom should emit a continuous spectrum. Instead atoms show sharp spectral lines (Balmer's hydrogen formula of 1885: λ⁻¹ = R(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²) was empirically known but unexplained).
New model
Bohr (1913), combining Rutherford's nucleus with Planck's quantum: electrons occupy only certain stationary orbits where angular momentum is quantized (mvr = nħ); radiation occurs only when jumping between orbits. Atomic spectra were explained for hydrogen — the first "old quantum theory" triumph. But it was a hybrid: classical orbits with an ad-hoc quantum rule bolted on, and it failed for multi-electron atoms.
En = −(mee⁴)/(2ħ²) · 1/n² = −13.6 eV/n²Bohr's hydrogen energy levels (1913) predict the Balmer series exactly. The Rydberg constant emerges from fundamental constants — a sensation.
Key papers
  • Rutherford E. The Scattering of α and β Particles by Matter and the Structure of the Atom. Phil. Mag. 21, 669 (1911). — Wikisource (free)
  • Bohr N. On the Constitution of Atoms and Molecules. Phil. Mag. 26, 1–25 (1913). — Wikisource (free)
41916–17 · Berlin — light quanta get statistical teeth

Einstein's A & B Coefficients

Old idea
Even Planck resisted Einstein's light quanta; emission and absorption of radiation were assumed to be continuous, directed processes.
The problem
What is the probability that an atom emits or absorbs light? Classical theory had no statistical description of radiation–matter exchange, and Planck's law itself lacked a deeper derivation.
New model
Einstein re-derived Planck's law from transition probabilities: spontaneous emission (A) and two forms of stimulated emission/absorption (B·ρ). Radiation pressure in emission implies the light quantum carries directed momentum p = hν/c — the photon concept grew stronger. His momentum-fluctuation argument (1924–25, with Bose) would finally give the photon its statistics.
A21/(B12ρ) balance gives ρ(ν) ∝ ν³/(ehν/kT − 1)  ·  p = hν/cThe A and B coefficients (1917). Stimulated emission underlies the laser (theoretically predicted 40 years before it was built).
51922–23 · The decisive experimental decade opens

Compton Scattering & the Stern–Gerlach Experiment

Old idea
Light scattering off electrons was thought to be a classical wave process (Thomson scattering); angular momentum in atoms was thought continuous, and orbital quantum numbers sufficed to label atomic states.
The problem
X-rays scattered off graphite showed a wavelength shift depending on the scattering angle — waves cannot change wavelength when scattered by a free electron. And the quantum theory of the atom needed direct proof that atomic states are spatially quantized.
New model & experiments
Compton (1923): X-ray scattering is a photon–electron collision obeying E = hν and p = hν/c exactly as in billiard-ball mechanics — the photon was experimentally proven, and even Planck finally accepted it. Stern–Gerlach (1922): a beam of silver atoms passing through an inhomogeneous magnetic field split into exactly two discrete spots — space quantization made visible to the naked eye. The two-way split was a puzzle: orbiting electrons should give an odd number of projections; the resolution (electron spin, Uhlenbeck–Goudsmit 1925, Pauli 1927) came only with the new quantum mechanics.
λ′ − λ = (h/mec)(1 − cos θ)Compton shift. The "Compton wavelength" h/mec ≈ 2.43 pm — a particle-collision formula written for light. Nobel Prize 1927.
61924 · Wave–particle duality formalized

de Broglie's Matter Waves & Bose–Einstein Statistics

Old idea
Waves (light) and particles (electrons) were distinct categories of being. Nuclei and electrons obeyed classical mechanics; photons had no statistical mechanics at all.
The problem
Bohr's quantization rule was arbitrary: why should angular momentum be mvr = nħ? What physical property of the electron picks out allowed orbits?
New model
Louis de Broglie (PhD thesis, 1924): if waves (photons) are particles, then particles are waves. Each electron carries a pilot wave of wavelength λ = h/p; the allowed Bohr orbits are exactly those that fit an integer number of wavelengths — quantization became a standing-wave condition. The same year, Satyendra Nath Bose derived Planck's law by treating light as a gas of identical quanta with a new counting method; Einstein extended it to particles (Bose–Einstein statistics) and predicted the Bose–Einstein condensate — achieved experimentally only in 1995.
λ = h/p   ·   ∮ p dq = nhde Broglie's relation (1924) and the Bohr–Sommerfeld quantization condition as a standing-wave requirement. Davisson–Germer (1927) confirmed electron diffraction; G.P. Thomson got the 1937 Nobel Prize with them.
71925–26 · The great reconstruction — quantum mechanics is born

Matrix Mechanics, Wave Mechanics & the Probability Interpretation

Old idea
The "old quantum theory" (Bohr–Sommerfeld): classical orbits plus ad-hoc quantum conditions. It worked for hydrogen, failed everywhere else (helium!, the anomalous Zeeman effect, intensities of spectral lines).
The problem
What replaces classical orbits? Heisenberg asked: keep only quantities that are actually observable — the frequencies and intensities of spectral lines. His multiplication rule turned out to be non-commutative — and Born recognized it as matrix multiplication. In the same year Pauli solved hydrogen with matrices, and Uhlenbeck–Goudsmit proposed electron spin. Then Schrödinger, from de Broglie waves, produced a completely different-looking wave equation — and proved the two theories mathematically equivalent.
New model
1926: Schrödinger's equation gives dynamics; Max Born adds the interpretation that |ψ|² is a probability density — the electron is not a particle riding a wave, and the wave does not "carry" energy in a classical sense. Determinism is gone at the fundamental level: nature gives only probabilities. Pauli's exclusion principle (1925, "Verbot") explains the periodic table and Fermi–Dirac statistics completes the two possible quantum statistics.
iħ ∂ψ/∂t = Ĥψ   ·   Ĥψ = Eψ   ·   x̂p̂ − p̂x̂ = iħSchrödinger's time-dependent and time-independent equations (1926), and the canonical commutation relation of Heisenberg's matrix mechanics (1925). Note the commutator: position and momentum no longer commute — the mathematical heart of quantum theory.
P(x) = |ψ(x)|² Born's statistical interpretation (1926). Nobel Prize 1954. Quantum states predict probabilities of measurement outcomes, not definite trajectories.
Key papers
  • Heisenberg W. Über quantentheoretische Umdeutung kinematischer… ZfP 33, 879 (1925) — matrix mechanics.
  • Schrödinger E. Quantisierung als Eigenwertproblem. Annalen der Physik 79 (1926). — Wikisource translation (free)
81927 · Copenhagen — the theory gets its meaning

Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle & the Solvay Confrontation

Old idea
Even after 1926, many (Schrödinger himself!) hoped the wavefunction described a real classical wave, and that a particle's position and momentum could both be sharply defined at once.
The problem
What does ψ mean? Schrödinger's wave packets spread; matrix mechanics had no "orbit" to speak of. The measurement process itself needed a principle. At the 1927 Solvay Conference, Einstein challenged Bohr with thought experiments (the single-slit photon box); Bohr defended the probabilistic reading. The debate shaped the Copenhagen interpretation.
New model
Heisenberg's uncertainty principle: position and momentum cannot both be known with arbitrary precision — not because of instrument imperfection, but because they are incompatible observables (non-commuting operators). Nature has a fundamental limit on simultaneous knowledge. Dirac's textbook (1930) and von Neumann's mathematical formalization (1932, Hilbert spaces) completed the framework.
Δx·Δp ≥ ħ/2   ·   ΔE·Δt ≥ ħ/2Heisenberg's uncertainty relations (1927). The wavefunction lives in a Hilbert space; observables are operators; measurement collapses the state.
91928 · Cambridge — quantum theory marries relativity

The Dirac Equation — and Antimatter Predicted

Old idea
Attempts to make quantum mechanics relativistic (Klein–Gordon equation, 1926) gave probabilities that could be negative and "pathological" solutions — Schrödinger had already tried and abandoned a relativistic wave equation in 1925. Pauli called it "the most physics-dead wrong" result.
The problem
Schrödinger's equation is not relativistic; Bohr's atom needs an explanation for fine structure (Sommerfeld's relativistic corrections) and for the electron's magnetic moment. The Klein–Gordon equation is second-order in time and its |ψ|² cannot be a probability density.
New model
Dirac demanded an equation first-order in time and relativistic — this forces the wavefunction to have four components and the Hamiltonian to be linear in the momentum. Spin and the electron's magnetic moment emerge automatically: spin is forced by relativity + quantum mechanics. But the equation also forced "negative energy" states. Dirac's bold move: the vacuum is a sea of filled negative-energy states, and a hole in the sea is a new particle with positive charge. Anderson discovered the positron in 1932 — the greatest prediction-to-discovery sequence in physics. (The "Dirac sea" was later reinterpreted by quantum field theory, where every particle has an antiparticle — but the equation, and the prediction, stand.)
(iγμ∂μ − m)ψ = 0   ·   {γμ, γν} = 2ημνDirac equation (1928). Its four-component wavefunction automatically carries spin-½ and gyromagnetic ratio g = 2. The negative-energy solutions → antimatter.

  • Dirac P.A.M. The quantum theory of the electron. Proc. R. Soc. A 117, 610–624 (1928). — DOI (Royal Society, free) · Publisher page
  • Anderson C.D. The apparent existence of easily deflectable positives. Science 76, 238 (1932) — positron discovery. — Science
101932–34 · The nucleus opens and beta decay breaks a law

Neutron, Positron, Neutrino & the Weak Interaction

Old idea
Nuclei were thought to contain protons and electrons (the "proton–electron" model); beta decay was assumed to be a two-body problem, so electrons should emerge with a single sharp energy.
The problem
Beta spectra are continuous — the electron emerges with any energy up to a maximum. Either energy conservation fails in single decays (Bohr briefly accepted this), or something invisible escapes. Also, nuclear "electrons" caused spin and statistics paradoxes (nitrogen-14), and repeated attempts to save classical pictures of nuclei failed. Chadwick's discovery of the neutron (1932) fixed the composition; the continuous beta spectrum demanded a new particle and a new force.
New model
Pauli (1930, "dear radioactive ladies and gentlemen") proposed the neutrino — a massless, chargeless, almost undetectable particle sharing the energy. Fermi (1933–34) built the first theory of beta decay: the neutron converts to a proton emitting an electron and an antineutrino via a new weak interaction, with a contact interaction (four-fermion coupling). Fermi's theory was a template for all later field theories; the neutrino was not detected until 1956 (Reines–Cowan).
n → p + e⁻ + ν̄e   ·   HFermi = GF (ψ̄pγμψn)(ψ̄eγμψν)Fermi's four-fermion beta-decay interaction (1934). The coupling GF was the prototype of a field-theory coupling; today this is an effective theory of the W boson interaction.
111930s · Quantum fields meet an infinity of trouble

QED's Infinite Divergences — the Crisis of the 1930s

Old idea
Dirac's 1927 quantum theory of radiation, and the quantization of the electromagnetic field, seemed like a natural next step: particles are quanta of fields; the electron self-energy and vacuum polarization should be calculable in QED, the theory of electrons + photons.
The problem
Every interesting quantity came out infinite. The electron's self-energy (interaction with its own field) diverges; vacuum polarization gives the vacuum an infinite charge density; the famous calculation of the Lamb-relevant atomic levels via perturbative QED all diverged logarithmically. Oppenheimer (1930), Heisenberg, Weisskopf and others diagnosed the divergences, but no one knew how to extract physical predictions. Many leading physicists (including Dirac) concluded QED was fundamentally broken. The war paused everything — then two microwave experiments restarted the crisis.
Eself ∝ ∫0e²/… d(k)/k → ∞   ·   δm → ∞Schematic: the electron's electromagnetic self-energy diverges at high momentum. The classical Abraham–Lorentz version was already divergent; QED made it logarithmic — but still infinite.
121947 · Shelter Island — the experiment that broke the stalemate

The Lamb Shift & the Anomalous Magnetic Moment

Old idea
Dirac's theory predicts that the 2s and 2p states of hydrogen are exactly degenerate; the electron's magnetic moment should be exactly one Bohr magneton (g = 2). These were considered exact predictions of the best theory available.
The problem
Using wartime microwave radar techniques, Willis Lamb and Robert Retherford (1947) measured the hydrogen fine structure and found a small energy splitting — about 1058 MHz — between 2s and 2p1/2: the Lamb shift. Almost simultaneously, Nafe, Nelson and Rabi's group found the electron's g-factor slightly different from 2. Dirac's "exact" theory was experimentally wrong at the level where QED's infinities were supposed to matter.
New model
At the Shelter Island Conference (June 1947), the young generation (Schwinger, Feynman, Dyson) decided the infinities could be tamed. Hans Bethe famously performed a non-relativistic calculation of the Lamb shift on the train home, getting the right order of magnitude by subtracting (renormalizing) the mass divergence. The physical idea: the electron's observed mass and charge already include all self-interaction effects; the divergences are unobservable bookkeeping, to be absorbed into a finite number of parameters.
ΔE(2s − 2p1/2) ≈ 1057.8 MHzLamb–Retherford (1947). Bethe's estimate: α⁵mec²·(4/3π)·ln(mec²/ΔEtyp) — the first finite, physical answer in QED.
Key papers
  • Lamb WE Jr, Retherford RC. Fine Structure of the Hydrogen Atom by a Microwave Method. Phys. Rev. 72, 241–243 (1947). — Physical Review (free)
  • Bethe HA. The Electromagnetic Shift of Energy Levels. Phys. Rev. 72, 339 (1947). — Physical Review (free)
131948–50 · The theory of light and matter is completed

Renormalized QED — Tomonaga, Schwinger, Feynman, Dyson

Old idea
Field theory was widely considered a dead end in the late 1940s; the divergences were thought to signal a fundamentally new physics at short distances. Bohr's institute discouraged Feynman from working on it.
The problem
How to extract finite predictions from a theory that computes infinities — systematically, relativistically, and to arbitrary precision? And how to connect the calculations to measurable quantities like the Lamb shift and g − 2?
New model
Working independently (Tomonaga in wartime Tokyo, Schwinger in Cambridge, Feynman in Cornell), three physicists constructed covariant QED and showed that all infinities can be absorbed into a finite set of renormalizations: the observed mass, charge, and wavefunction. Freeman Dyson (1948–49) proved the three approaches equivalent and showed the renormalized perturbation series is systematically finite order by order. Feynman's diagrams turned the machinery into a visual calculus; Schwinger's operator methods computed g − 2 to extraordinary precision. The anomalous magnetic moment of the electron, ae = (g − 2)/2 ≈ α/2π, was computed by Schwinger and matched experiment — the first precision triumph of a quantum field theory.
ae = (g − 2)/2 = α/(2π) ≈ 0.00116Schwinger's famous 1948 result — the electron interacts with its own quantum field, shifting g from the Dirac value 2. Modern QED agrees with the measured g to 12+ decimal places — the most precisely verified theory in science.
L(ψ, A) = ψ̄(iγμDμ − m)ψ − ¼FμνFμνThe QED Lagrangian: Dμ = ∂μ + ieAμ. All quantum electrodynamics — atoms, chemistry, light — follows from quantizing this expression. The "final form" that the 1900–1950 revolution produced.

  • Feynman RP. Space-Time Approach to Quantum Electrodynamics. Phys. Rev. 76, 769 (1949). — Physical Review (free)
