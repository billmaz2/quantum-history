Transposon History
Genetics · 1944–2022

Transposon History

How spotted maize kernels led to genes that cut and paste, to genes that copy themselves through RNA, to the Alu repeat that fills a tenth of the human genome, and to the 2001 draft genome that showed how much of our DNA came from them. Every step names the experiment and the paper, with a DOI that resolves.

Terms used below
Transposable element any
A stretch of DNA that can change its position in a genome. Also called a transposon or mobile element.
DNA transposon Class II
Moves as DNA by cut and paste, using an enzyme it encodes (transposase). Usually ends in terminal inverted repeats. McClintock's Ac and Ds are the classic case.
Retrotransposon Class I
Makes an RNA copy, reverse-transcribes it to DNA and inserts the new copy. The original stays put, so copy number goes up.
LTR retrotransposon Class I
Has long terminal repeats and resembles a retrovirus. Yeast Ty1 is an example. Endogenous retroviruses belong here.
LINE Class I
Long interspersed element, a non-LTR retrotransposon that encodes its own reverse transcriptase. Human L1 is about 6 kb.
SINE Class I
Short interspersed element. It encodes no proteins and borrows the machinery of a LINE. Alu is the human SINE.
Autonomous / non-autonomous
An autonomous element encodes what it needs to move (Ac, L1). A non-autonomous one depends on another element's enzyme (Ds, Alu).
Target site duplication
A short repeat of host DNA on each side of an insertion, left by the way the element is joined in.

Chapter 1 · Class II

McClintock and the spotted kernels

Why do some maize kernels carry purple spots, and why does the spotting differ from ear to ear?

Observation

McClintock studied maize cytogenetics at Cold Spring Harbor from 1944. She followed unstable alleles that were mutant in one cell and normal in its descendants. Kernels with the colourless allele showed purple sectors, as if a mutation had been reversed in single cells and passed on.

The sector's size recorded when the change happened. An early event gave a large sector and a late event a speck. The control panel at the top of this page lets you change the rate and see that logic.

Experiment · 1950

Ds only acts when Ac is present, and both move

On chromosome 9, McClintock found a site, Ds (Dissociation), where the chromosome broke. Breaks occurred only if a second element, Ac (Activator), was in the genome. Both could change position. When Ds sat inside a pigment gene it blocked the gene, and when it left, the gene worked again in that cell and its descendants. She called them controlling elements and saw them as regulators of gene action.

What it proved: parts of the genome are mobile, and one element can control another's movement.
McClintock, PNAS 36:344–355 (1950) [1] · Cold Spring Harbor Symposium (1951) [2]
Experiment · 1983

Cloning Ac and Ds

Thirty years later Fedoroff, Wessler and Shure isolated the elements as DNA. Ac is an autonomous element. Ds elements are shorter versions that lost the middle of the sequence and so cannot move alone. That matches her genetic result: Ds moves only when Ac supplies the enzyme. McClintock received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1983, alone, for the discovery of mobile genetic elements.

What it proved: controlling elements are specific pieces of DNA, with an autonomous member and non-autonomous relatives.
Fedoroff, Wessler & Shure, Cell 35:235–242 (1983) [3]
Concept · 1984

In her 1984 Science paper McClintock argued that the genome responds to stress by activating and restructuring its mobile parts, which she called genomic shock. How general that is remains debated. The paper is still the clearest statement of her view. [4] For retrospectives, see Fedoroff and Ravindran, both from 2012. [5] [6]

Chapter 2 · Class I

Copying through RNA

If an element copies itself, where does the copy come from?

Concept · 1964–70

Temin's provirus hypothesis said that RNA tumour viruses make a DNA copy of their genome. In 1970 two groups found the enzyme. Baltimore and Temin with Mizutani each reported an RNA-dependent DNA polymerase in virus particles. [7] [8] Information could flow from RNA back into DNA, and a genome could in principle gain sequences that way.

Experiment · 1985

Boeke and Fink: the intron trick in yeast

Yeast Ty elements look like retroviruses without an envelope. Boeke, Garfinkel, Styles and Fink put an intron into a Ty element, in the orientation that is removed only from the RNA transcript, and put the element under an inducible promoter. Transposed copies had lost the intron. Only an RNA intermediate could explain that.

What it proved: Ty elements move through an RNA intermediate, so they are retrotransposons, relatives of retroviruses that stay inside the cell.
Boeke, Garfinkel, Styles & Fink, Cell 40:491–500 (1985) [9]

Cut-and-paste versus copy-and-paste

Press Transpose a few times on each track. Real genomes are more complicated, so see the note below.

DNA transposon (cut and paste)
Retrotransposon (copy and paste)

A cut-and-paste element can still gain copies. If it jumps from a region that has already replicated into one that has not, or if the cell repairs the gap it left by copying the sister chromosome, the count rises. A retrotransposon adds a copy on every successful event, which is why they build up to hundreds of thousands of copies.

Chapter 3 · LINEs

L1: the engine in our genome

Which elements are still moving in people, and how?

Observation · 1988

Kazazian and colleagues studied the factor VIII gene in patients with haemophilia A and found L1 sequences inserted into the gene in patients, not present in the parents' copies of it. A mobile element in the human genome had moved recently enough to cause a new mutation.

What it proved: L1 is still active in humans, and retrotransposition is a source of new disease mutations.
Kazazian et al., Nature 332:164–166 (1988) [10]
Experiment · 1993–96

How a LINE inserts: target-primed reverse transcription

Luan, Korman, Jakubczak and Eickbush studied R2, a non-LTR element of the silkworm that inserts into ribosomal DNA. Its protein cuts one strand of the target and uses the exposed end as a primer to reverse-transcribe the element's RNA directly onto the chromosome. [11] In 1996 Feng and colleagues showed human L1 encodes an endonuclease needed for retrotransposition, [13] and Moran and colleagues built the cell assay that made L1 testable. They split a reporter gene with an intron, the same logic as Boeke's yeast experiment, so cells survive selection only after the full cycle of transcription, splicing, reverse transcription and insertion. [12]

Luan et al., Cell 72:595–605 (1993) · Moran et al., Cell 87:917–927 (1996) · Feng et al., Cell 87:905–916 (1996)

Chapter 4 · SINEs

Alu: a short repeat that rides on L1

What is the most common transposon in the human genome, and where did it come from?

Discovery · 1980

By the late 1970s, reassociation studies showed human DNA contained a very abundant family of repeats about 300 base pairs long. Jelinek and colleagues described it as a family of interspersed repeats found throughout mammalian genomes. It was named Alu after the AluI restriction site that cuts most of its members. [14] Schmid and Jelinek reviewed what was known two years later. [15]

Experiment · 1984

Alu is a processed 7SL RNA gene

Ullu and Tschudi compared Alu with the gene for 7SL RNA, part of the signal recognition particle. Alu has the features of a dimer of 7SL RNA with a central stretch missing, so it began as a copy of that gene made through RNA.

What it proved: Alu is not a virus-like element. It is a retroposed cellular RNA gene that became a parasite of the retrotransposition machinery.
Ullu & Tschudi, Nature 312:171–172 (1984) [16]
Experiment · 2003

Alu uses L1's enzyme

Alu encodes no proteins. Dewannieux, Esnault and Heidmann marked human Alu elements with a reporter that works only after retrotransposition. Cells that made L1's proteins mobilised the marked Alus, and cells without them did not.

What it proved: Alu is a non-autonomous SINE that depends on LINE proteins, like Ds depending on Ac, but through RNA.
Dewannieux, Esnault & Heidmann, Nat Genet 35:41–48 (2003) [18]

Anatomy of an Alu insertion

Approximate proportions. Two related halves, an A-rich linker, a poly(A) tail, and the target site duplications on either side.

What it did next

Alu elements arose early in primate evolution and number over a million in the human genome, about a tenth of its sequence. They fall into subfamilies of different ages, and the youngest still insert and create polymorphisms between people that are used to study ancestry. Because there are so many similar copies, Alu elements can also pair up wrongly during recombination and cause deletions or duplications. Batzer and Deininger review all of this. [17]

Chapter 5 · Genomes

What the 2001 genome showed

How much of our DNA is transposon-derived?

Result · 2001

In February 2001 the International Human Genome Sequencing Consortium published its draft in Nature, and Celera published its own, built by whole-genome shotgun sequencing, in Science. [19] [20] The consortium's analysis recognised that about 45% of the sequence was derived from transposable elements. LINEs were the largest share, then SINEs, then LTR elements, then DNA transposons.

Most copies are old and degraded, so the genome reads like a fossil record. DNA transposons and LTR elements were largely inactive for tens of millions of years in the human lineage. L1 and Alu were the main elements still active.

Since then

In 2011 de Koning and colleagues searched the genome without relying on known families, and estimated that repeats make up at least about two-thirds of it. [21] In 2022 the T2T consortium reported the first complete human genome, closing the gaps that the 2001 drafts left in repeat-rich regions, and a companion paper described the transcription and chromatin state of the repeat elements in it. [22] [23]

Chapter 6 · Host and function

Living with transposons

Are they only parasites?

Control

Host

Cells silence transposons. In the Drosophila germline, clusters of transposon fragments produce piRNAs that guide silencing of matching elements. Brennecke and colleagues mapped these loci in 2007. DNA methylation and chromatin marks also silence elements in plants and mammals. [26]

Domestication

Function

Some transposon genes were adopted by the host. Syncytin, from a retroviral envelope gene, is involved in human placental development. [27] RAG1 and RAG2, which assemble antibody and T-cell receptor genes, can transpose DNA, which points to a transposon ancestor. [28]

Mutation and mosaicism

Host

New L1 and Alu insertions occasionally disrupt genes and cause disease. [10] L1 can also act in somatic cells. Muotri and colleagues reported retrotransposition in neural progenitors. [29]

Classification

Genomes

Wicker and colleagues proposed a unified system that splits elements into Class I and Class II and then into orders and families by how they transpose. [24] Wells and Feschotte give a current tour of the diversity. [25]
