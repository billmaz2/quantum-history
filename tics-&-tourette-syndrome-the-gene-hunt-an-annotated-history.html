1 · Why Tourette genetics is hard

Tourette syndrome (TS) — chronic multiple motor and vocal tics, defined by Georges Gilles de la Tourette in 1885 — is one of the most heritable neuropsychiatric disorders, yet for thirty years its genes refused to be found. The reason is now textbook: inheritance is complex — many loci of small or rare effect, incomplete penetrance, variable expressivity (tics, OCD, ADHD in the same families), and no single "Tourette gene" that is necessary or sufficient [1].

Every method of human genetics has therefore been tried on TS in sequence — segregation analysis, parametric linkage, affected-sib-pair scans, cytogenetic positional cloning, candidate-gene association driven by the dopamine pharmacology of tic suppression, then GWAS, copy-number variants and trio exome sequencing. Each wave taught the field something, and the story of how each finding was made is as instructive as the findings themselves.

2 · Timeline of the gene hunt

  • 1885 — Gilles de la Tourette describes nine patients with the tic disorder Charcot names after him ("maladie des tics"; Arch Gén Méd 1885, cited bibliographically below — predates digital indexing) clinical description
  • 1960s–80s — The neuroleptic haloperidol is found to suppress tics — a D2 dopamine receptor blockade — founding the dopamine hypothesis that later nominated the first candidate genes; family, twin and adoption studies establish strong heritability; segregation analyses in large pedigrees (Pauls, Kidd, Leckman and colleagues) propose an autosomal-dominant major gene — the model that motivated the first genome scans pharmacology / biochemistry · segregation analysis
  • 1991 — The candidate-gene era opens: Comings and colleagues report association of the dopamine D2 receptor (DRD2) locus with TS and related disorders [3]; dozens of studies follow on DRD2, DRD4 (Grice et al., transmission-disequilibrium test), DAT1, MAO-A (VNTR) and DBH — mostly small samples, mostly inconsistent replication candidate-gene association (case-control & TDT)
  • 1996 — Cytogenetics enters: Boghosian-Sell and colleagues describe a TS pedigree with a 7;18 translocation and map the 18q22.3 breakpoint — the first TS-relevant chromosomal rearrangement to be cloned regionally [4] cytogenetics
  • 1999 — The first whole-genome scans: Barr and colleagues screen 386 markers across multigenerational families — no significant parametric linkage; and the TSA International Consortium completes a genome screen in 110 affected sib pairs, with only suggestive (multipoint MLS ~1–2) signals [5], [6] parametric linkage · affected-sib-pair genome scan
  • 2000 — A large French-Canadian pedigree yields one of the first significant parametric LOD scores in TS — a three-point Zmax 3.24 on chromosome 11 (D11S1377–D11S933) [7], but the signal does not generalize to other families parametric linkage, single pedigree
  • 2001 — IMMP2L — the first TS gene found by positional cloning from a chromosome rearrangement: Petek, Kroisel, Wagner and colleagues characterize a patient with tics and a rearranged 7q31 and show the breakpoint disrupts a novel gene, IMMP2L (inner mitochondrial membrane peptidase-like) [8]; an independent t(2;7)(p24.2;q31) translocation case disrupting IMMP2L follows in 2011 [9] — rare but repeatedly implicated cytogenetics → positional cloning
  • 2005 — SLITRK1 — the first widely publicized TS gene: Abelson, State and colleagues, studying a child with TS and a de novo inversion at 13q31.1, find the neurite-growth gene SLITRK1 at the breakpoint; they then find rare coding and 3′UTR variants (var321, varCDfs) enriched in TS cases, and show SLITRK1 promotes dendritic growth in neurons [10]. Subsequent screens find the variants in very few patients — an object lesson in the winner's curse and the need for replication cytogenetics + candidate sequencing + functional assay
  • 2007 — The largest linkage study of the era: the TSAICG scans 238 families / 304 affected sib pairs plus 18 multigenerational families — no single locus reaches genome-wide significance, confirming the polygenic architecture [11] nonparametric + parametric multi-family genome scan
  • 2010 — HDC — the definitive single-family gene: in a two-generation pedigree (a father and eight children, all affected), Ercan-Sencicek, State, Leckman and colleagues combine linkage analysis with candidate-gene sequencing and find a rare segregating nonsense mutation, W317X in histidine decarboxylase — the rate-limiting enzyme of histamine biosynthesis — introducing the histamine pathway into tic biology [12] linkage + sequencing in one pedigree
  • 2013 — The first GWAS (TSAICG): 1,285 TS cases and 4,964 controls, including Ashkenazi and French-Canadian population isolates — no marker reaches genome-wide significance; top signal rs7868992 near COL27A1 on 9q32 (P = 1.85 × 10⁻⁶) [13] GWAS
  • 2014 — Biochemistry closes the loop on HDC: Baldan Castellan, Pittenger and colleagues show Hdc knockout mice develop tic-like stereotypies, have elevated striatal dopamine, and both mice and human mutation carriers show altered D2/D3 binding — the clearest mechanistic path from a TS gene to a circuit [14] biochemistry & mouse model
  • 2017 — Two breakthroughs: (i) the TSAICG CNV study of 2,434 cases vs 4,093 controls finds the first genome-wide significant structural variants — NRXN1 deletions (OR ≈ 20) and CNTN6 duplications (OR ≈ 10), carried by ~1% of cases [15]; and (ii) the TIC Genetics consortium sequences 511 trios and shows de novo damaging coding variants are strongly associated with TS, implicating specific genes across synaptic, chromatin and histaminergic pathways [16] CNV analysis · trio whole-exome sequencing
  • 2019 — The first genome-wide significant SNP locus: a GWAS meta-analysis of 4,819 cases and 9,488 controls (plus a population-based replication sample) identifies rs2504235 within FLT3 on chromosome 13 — a start toward common-variant risk architecture, together with strong polygenic signal and cross-disorder correlation with OCD [17] GWAS meta-analysis
  • 2023 — The GWAS era matures: the largest meta-analysis to date (≈6,133 TS cases, 13,565 controls) finds a novel genome-wide significant locus on 5q15 upstream of NR2F1 — a nuclear orphan receptor involved in cortical development [18] GWAS meta-analysis

3 · The discovery methods, explained

Segregation analysis

Statistical modelling of inheritance patterns in pedigrees. In TS it suggested an autosomal-dominant major gene — which was too simple, but justified the first linkage searches. This method tells you what kind of gene to look for, not where it is.

Linkage analysis & whole-genome scans

Typed ~300–400 polymorphic markers across the genome in families, testing whether a marker co-segregates with illness (parametric LOD score, needing a correct inheritance model) or is shared identical-by-descent among affected sibs (model-free). TS linkage signals (11q, 4q, 5p, 8p…) were consistently suggestive and inconsistent between studies — the classic signature of many small effects [6], [11].

Cytogenetics & positional cloning

Patients with tics and rare chromosomal rearrangements (translocations, inversions, duplications) act as "experiments of nature": the disrupted gene at the breakpoint is a candidate. This is how IMMP2L (7q31 breakpoints [8], [9]) and SLITRK1 (de novo 13q31.1 inversion [10]) were nominated.

Candidate-gene association

Pick genes from pharmacology — haloperidol suppresses tics, so dopamine genes (DRD2, DRD4, DAT1, MAO-A, DBH) — and compare allele frequencies in cases vs controls (or with the transmission-disequilibrium test in trios). Dozens of studies; associations reported but rarely confirmed [3]. The lasting result: dopaminergic genes remain plausible modifiers, not confirmed susceptibility loci.

GWAS

Test 500,000+ common SNPs genome-wide in thousands of cases and controls. In TS: the first scan (2013) found nothing significant [13]; the 2019 meta-analysis delivered the first significant SNP (FLT3, rs2504235 [17]) and the 2023 meta-analysis added NR2F1 on 5q15 [18].

CNV analysis

Microarrays detect deletions and duplications of DNA chunks. TS shows an excess of large, rare, de novo CNVs, with genome-wide significance for NRXN1 deletions and CNTN6 duplications — each carried by ~1% of cases [15].

Trio exome sequencing

Sequence the protein-coding genome of child-plus-parents ("trio"); variants present in the child but not either parent are de novo. In 511 TS trios, damaging de novo coding variants are strongly enriched, and multiple genes are hit more than once across cohorts — the modern route to rare high-impact TS genes [16].

Biochemistry & functional validation

The dopamine hypothesis began as pure pharmacology (haloperidol → tic suppression → D2 receptor). The HDC discovery was validated biochemically: the W317X enzyme is inactive; Hdc-knockout mice show tic-like stereotypies, elevated striatal dopamine, altered D2/D3 binding — rescued by haloperidol and histamine [14]. Similarly SLITRK1 was vetted in dendritic-growth assays [10].

The consensus picture: TS is polygenic (many common variants of small effect, now measurable as polygenic risk scores), plus a rare-variant tail (CNVs and de novo coding variants), converging on cortico-striatal circuit development — synapse adhesion (NRXN1, CNTN6, SLITRK1), histamine signalling (HDC), dopaminergic modulation, and chromatin regulation [1], [16].
