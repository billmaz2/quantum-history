Introduction

Between 1850 and 1950, biochemistry moved from grinding tissues in kitchen-chemistry solvents to reading the molecular language of heredity. This page follows that arc in three parallel stories: (1) the isolation of "nuclein" and its transformation into "nucleic acid" and finally into DNA and RNA; (2) the chemical dissection of the bases, sugars, and nucleotides; and (3) the discovery and analytical isolation of the amino acids. Each milestone links the experimenter, the method, the primary paper, and modern PubMed and Google Scholar entry points.

A note on links: papers before ~1920 are usually not indexed in PubMed as full records. For those, PubMed and Google Scholar search links are provided; for 20th-century classics, direct article links and DOIs are given where they exist.

1850 Adolph Strecker amino acids

First chemical synthesis of an amino acid: Strecker converts acetaldehyde + HCN + ammonia into alanine, proving amino acids are ordinary organic molecules — the conceptual foundation for treating proteins as polymers of defined chemistry.

Felix Hoppe-Seyler establishes physiological chemistry as a discipline and isolates haemoglobin in crystalline form; his school invents the routine methods of protein precipitation (heat, acids, alcohol, salts) that Miescher would push to their limits.

Working in Hoppe-Seyler's Tübingen lab, Miescher extracts from pus-cell (leucocyte) nuclei a phosphorus-rich, acid substance resistant to pepsin: he names it "nuclein." This is the birth of nucleic-acid chemistry. He later obtains it in high yield from salmon sperm, exploiting the fact that sperm heads are almost pure nucleus.

Miescher and his circle detect guanine and other purine-like material in sperm nuclein; nuclein is no longer seen as a single substance but as a complex of protein with a non-protein, phosphorus-containing acid that carries "xanthine bodies" (purines).

Kossel begins the systematic chemical dissection of nuclein. He isolates from yeast and pancreatic extracts the base he names "adenine" (from Greek aden, gland), and in 1884 the protein fraction he calls "histones." He shows nuclein = protein + an acid rich in purines.

Methods — Isolating Nucleic Acids (1869 → 1950)

Miescher's alkaline extraction (1868–69)

  1. Wash pus cells from surgical bandages with dilute sodium sulfate solution to remove serum proteins.
  2. Extract cells with dilute HCl overnight — the cytoplasm dissolves, leaving naked nuclei.
  3. Treat nuclei with dilute NaOH; neutralize with acetic acid → a grey, stringy precipitate: nuclein (DNA + protein).
  4. Control test: nuclein resists gastric pepsin in acid, proving it is not ordinary protein.

Logic: differential solubility + enzyme resistance = a new chemical category. (Salmon-sperm variant: Miescher later used the pure nuclei of sperm heads to get grams of material.)

Altmann's "nucleic acid" deproteinization (1889)

  1. Digest tissue with pepsin in 0.1% HCl at 37–40 °C to destroy protein.
  2. Extract the residue with dilute alkali; the nucleic acid dissolves.
  3. Precipitate the acid with glacial acetic acid / alcohol → protein-free nucleic acid.

Logic: enzymatic removal of protein frees the non-protein moiety — the conceptual split of "nuclein" into nucleic acid + protein (histone in chromosomes).

Avery–MacLeod–McCarty DNA purification (1944)

  1. Lyse heat-killed Type III pneumococci; precipitate DNA with ethanol; wind fibres on a spindle.
  2. Deproteinize by shaking with chloroform (protein denatures at the interface).
  3. Digest with purified enzymes as controls: DNase destroys activity; protease and RNase do not.
  4. Activity tracks with the chemistry of DNA (negative Schlieren, orcinol and diphenylamine reactions, 260 nm absorption).

Logic: enzyme ablation + chemical tests = an identity claim for the biological activity. This template — purify, then knock out with a specific enzyme — remains the core of molecular evidence.

RNA/DNA quantitative separation (1945)

  • Schmidt–Thannhauser: alkaline hydrolysis at 37 °C; RNA phosphorus becomes acid-soluble nucleotides, DNA phosphorus does not → subtract to get RNA-P and DNA-P.
  • Schneider: extract the tissue with hot 5% trichloroacetic acid — both nucleic acids dissolve, proteins stay precipitated; then estimate by color reactions (diphenylamine for DNA, orcinol for RNA).

Methods — Isolating Bases & Nucleotides

Kossel's school: hydrolysis + crystalline derivatives (1885–1900)

  1. Acid hydrolysis of nucleic acid (HCl, formic acid) liberates the bases.
  2. Exploit solubility differences: precipitate purines as picrates or silver nitrate salts in ammoniacal solution; crystallize free bases from solution.
  3. Confirm identity by elemental analysis, melting point, and comparison with known purines (Fischer's synthetic guanine, adenine).

Logic: break the polymer → capture small molecules as crystalline salts → prove structure by comparison with synthesized standards. This is 19th-century "gravimetric-organic" analysis.

Levene: dissecting the nucleotide (1909–1929)

  • Gentle hydrolysis of yeast nucleic acid with dilute ammonia yields crystalline nucleosides and nucleotides (e.g., inosinic acid → hypoxanthine + ribose + phosphate).
  • Sugar released by cleavage; identified as D-ribose (1909) and later 2-deoxy-D-ribose (1929) by optical rotation and osazone derivatives.
  • Conclusion: the monomer is base–sugar–phosphate; the polymer is a chain of these units.

Chromatographic revolution (1944–1950)

  • Paper partition chromatography: spot hydrolysate on filter paper, develop with solvent (e.g., butanol–HCl), locate bases under a UV lamp (dark-quenching spots at 260 nm), elute and read quantities spectrophotometrically (Vischer & Chargaff).
  • Ion-exchange chromatography (Cohn, Oak Ridge): anion-exchange resins separate nucleotides by charge/charge distribution — the basis of the later nucleotide maps and of Moore–Stein amino-acid columns.

Methods — Isolating Amino Acids

19th century: hydrolysis + fractional crystallization (1820–1900)

  1. Acid hydrolysis of proteins with concentrated HCl (Braconnot's approach, first applied to gelatin, 1820) breaks the chain into free amino acids.
  2. Separate by fractional crystallization of salts (copper, barium) — how glycine, leucine, tyrosine, cystine, etc., were discovered one at a time.
  3. Identify by elemental analysis and comparison with synthetic samples (Strecker synthesis as proof of structure).

Fischer's ester distillation (1900–1906)

  1. Esterify the amino acids with ethanol + HCl.
  2. Fractional vacuum distillation of the ethyl esters separates even homologues that crystallize together.
  3. Hydrolyse each ester fraction back to the free amino acid; count and identify all residues of a protein.

Logic: convert polar, non-volatile molecules into volatile derivatives — physical separation supersedes crystallization luck.

Quantitation & modern analysis (1907–1950)

  • Van Slyke gasometric method (1911–1914): nitrous acid liberates N₂ from free α-amino groups — free amino nitrogen becomes measurable during hydrolysis.
  • Microbiological assays (1940s, Snell): lactic-acid bacteria that require a given amino acid convert growth into a titration curve.
  • Paper chromatography + ninhydrin (Consden–Gordon–Martin, 1944): two-dimensional maps of amino acids; Sanger uses it to read the sequence of insulin peptides (1945–1951).
  • Ion-exchange columns (Moore & Stein, 1949–1951): complete automatic amino-acid composition in one run; by 1958, the first amino-acid analyzer.

DNA vs RNA — How the Difference Was Found, and How the Two Were Separated

Today "DNA" and "RNA" look like two obvious categories, but for forty years biochemists knew only "plant nucleic acid" (yeast) and "animal nucleic acid" (thymus). The recognition that there are exactly two chemically distinct nucleic acids — and then the discovery that both occur in every cell — came from an interplay of chemical, enzymatic, and cytological evidence.

Stage 1 — Two nucleic acids by chemistry (1900–1929)

  • Hydrolysis products differed. Kossel's and Levene's analyses showed yeast nucleic acid yields the pentose D-ribose plus uracil, while thymus nucleic acid yields a sugar that resisted identification for twenty years plus thymine (Levene & Mori, 1929: 2-deoxy-D-ribose). The sugars were the decisive discriminator: identical bases except uracil↔thymine, but different carbohydrates.
  • Optical and colour tests. Orcinol (Bial's) reaction detects pentoses generally; the diphenylamine reaction (Dische, 1930) became the specific colorimetric test for the deoxyribose of DNA — still the classic DNA assay. Feulgen's Nuklealreaktion (1914/1924 with Rossenbeck) rests on the same chemistry: mild acid hydrolysis strips purines and frees aldehyde groups on deoxyribose, which react with Schiff reagent to give a purple colour only with DNA.
  • Physical behaviour differed. Thymus "sodium nucleinate" formed extremely viscous, threads-on-stirring-rod solutions and gelled on warming; yeast nucleic acid did not — an early hint of the enormous size and secondary structure of DNA, before anyone could measure a molecular weight.

False conclusion of the era: because DNA was found in animal cells and RNA in plant/yeast cells, textbooks taught "animal = DNA, plant = RNA." Both ideas died in the 1930s–40s, below.

Stage 2 — Enzymes prove the two are distinct and coexist (1903–1948)

  • Distinct nucleases. A DNase ("nucleodepolymerase") was described by Levene (1905); ribonuclease was detected in the 1920s (Jones, 1920) and crystallized by Kunitz (1940) from pancreas. Enzyme specificity is chemistry's sharpest scalpel: an enzyme that destroys RNA but leaves DNA intact is itself proof that the two substrates differ, and a tool to remove one to study the other.
  • Both nucleic acids in every cell. Once RNase and DNase were available, extracts of any tissue could be digested selectively — RNA and DNA turned out to coexist in all animal and plant cells (confirmed quantitatively by Schmidt & Thannhauser, Davidson, and Chargaff's group in the 1940s: DNA concentrated in chromosomes, RNA in cytoplasm and nucleolus).
  • Avery 1944 & Hershey–Chase 1952: DNase (not RNase, not protease) destroys pneumococcal transformation; ³²P (DNA) but not ³⁵S (protein) enters infected bacteria — the functional proof that the deoxyribo-type polymer is the genetic material.

Stage 3 — Cytological methods: seeing DNA and RNA in the cell

Feulgen reaction (1914, Feulgen & Rossenbeck 1924)

  1. Fix tissue section; hydrolyse gently in 1 M HCl at 60 °C (~8 min): apurinic acid forms, freeing deoxyribose aldehydes.
  2. Rinse and treat with colourless Schiff reagent (fuchsin decolourized by bisulphite).
  3. Purple-magenta product appears only where DNA is; RNA does not react because its ribose has no free aldehyde at C2 under these conditions.
  4. Controls: DNase pretreatment abolishes the stain; RNase does not.

Result: DNA localized to chromosomes; in Feulgen's hands this killed the idea that DNA was only animal — it stained plant chromosomes too. Combined with microphotometry (microscope photometer measures the dye amount per nucleus), the Feulgen method gave the first quantitative DNA-per-nucleus data and showed DNA content is constant per species and halved in gametes — cytological proof of DNA's genetic role before 1944.

UV microspectrophotometry (Caspersson, 1936–1940)

  • Nucleic acids absorb 260 nm (the bases), proteins absorb 280 nm (aromatic amino acids).
  • Caspersson built a UV microscope with a photometer and measured single living cells: intense 260-nm absorption in chromosomes (DNA) and in the nucleolus and cytoplasm (RNA); protein-rich regions shifted the ratio to 280 nm.
  • With Jean Brachet, the picture emerged: chromosomes = DNA + protein, nucleolus/cytoplasm = RNA + protein, and RNA content correlates with protein synthesis (pancreas, silk gland, growing oocyte).
Key paper
Caspersson, T. (1936) Skand. Arch. Physiol. 78, Suppl. 8. Google Scholar

Unna–Pappenheim / methyl green–pyronine (Brachet, 1940–1943)

  1. Stain the section with a mixture of two basic dyes: methyl green and pyronine Y.
  2. Methyl green binds preferentially to polymerized DNA (blue-green nuclei); pyronine binds RNA (red nucleolus and cytoplasm).
  3. Enzyme controls (Brachet's decisive step): pre-digest the section with crystallized ribonuclease — the red stain vanishes while the green nuclear stain survives; DNase does the reverse. This pairing of staining with nuclease digestion made the method specific and convinced the field that RNA is a general constituent of all cells.

Stage 4 — Biochemical methods: separating DNA from RNA in a test tube

Schmidt & Thannhauser alkaline fractionation (1945)

  1. Extract the tissue and remove "acid-soluble" phosphorus (small nucleotides, inorganic phosphate) with cold TCA; lipids with alcohol/ether.
  2. Incubate the residue in 0.3–1 M NaOH, 37 °C, overnight: the 2′-OH of RNA allows alkaline transesterification → RNA breaks into acid-soluble nucleotides (RNA-P); DNA, lacking the 2′-OH, survives intact.
  3. Acidify: DNA precipitates (plus phosphoprotein), RNA nucleotides stay in solution → measure RNA-P and DNA-P separately by phosphorus analysis.

Conceptual core: the whole method exploits the one chemical difference between the sugars — the 2′-hydroxyl. Alkali is not just a reagent; it is a structural probe.

Schneider hot-TCA extraction (1945) & Ogur–Rosen perchloric acid (1950)

  • Schneider: extract both nucleic acids from the protein with 5% TCA at 90 °C; estimate DNA by the diphenylamine reaction and RNA by orcinol in the same extract.
  • Ogur & Rosen: differential extraction with hot perchloric acid — first RNA (10 min, 70 °C, mild acid), then DNA from the residue (15 min, 90 °C), minimizing cross-contamination.
  • Chargaff's route: hydrolyse total nucleic acid with formic acid, resolve the four bases by paper chromatography (two solvent systems), quantify each eluted base by UV absorption — the first reliable base-composition tables, which also revealed RNA and DNA compositions as independently variable.

Selective enzymatic digestion & differential precipitation

  • Digest purified total nucleic acid with crystallized RNase; precipitate undigested DNA with acid/alcohol — a preparative, not just analytical, separation.
  • Differential solubility: DNA "sodium nucleinate" precipitates as fibres in ~0.1 M NaCl + ethanol; RNA precipitates more slowly as a gelatinous mass. Guanine-rich RNA (later called "RNA gel") forms insoluble complexes with copper salts — 19th-century-style separations that still work at the bench.
  • Epilogue beyond 1950: the logic culminates in Kirby's phenol/SDS extraction (1956) where DNA stays in the aqueous phase and protein goes to phenol, Mirsky's DNA-histone dissociation with strong salt, Meselson–Stahl density-gradient centrifugation (1957, CsCl) resolving DNA species by buoyant density (GC-rich DNA is denser), and the separation of RNA by sucrose gradients — the direct descendants of the Schmidt–Thannhauser idea: make the two polymers behave differently, then pull them apart.

How Schmidt & Thannhauser and Schneider actually reasoned — the thinking behind the two methods

The problem both faced (early 1940s): tissue extracts contain DNA, RNA, free nucleotides, phosphoproteins, inorganic phosphate and phospholipids — all carrying phosphorus — yet the only universally available, sub-microgram quantitative detector available in every lab was phosphorus analysis (the Fiske–Subbarow colorimetric method, 1925) and a handful of colour reactions. There were no specific stains in a test tube, no spectrophotometers in most labs, and no way to physically separate intact DNA from intact RNA at scale. The question was therefore not "how do I purify the two polymers?" but "how do I convert the difference in their chemistry into a difference in a number I can measure?"

Schmidt & Thannhauser's chain of reasoning (1945):

  1. Known chemistry as a lever: organic chemists had known since ~1900 that alkali rapidly destroys yeast nucleic acid but leaves thymus nucleic acid essentially intact. Why: the 2′-OH of RNA's ribose allows an intramolecular transesterification — the phosphate backbone cyclizes and the chain collapses into small nucleotides; DNA has no 2′-OH, so there is nothing for the phosphate to cyclize onto, and the backbone survives alkali.
  2. Convert structure into solubility: intact polymers are acid-insoluble (they precipitate with acid); small nucleotides are acid-soluble. So after alkaline incubation: acidify → RNA fragments stay dissolved in the supernatant ("RNA-P"), DNA (plus phosphoprotein) precipitates ("DNA-P").
  3. Subtraction accounting: first wash out the small-molecule phosphorus with cold TCA before hydrolysis (otherwise free nucleotides would contaminate the RNA fraction), remove lipids with alcohol/ether, then split the residue into two fractions and measure each by phosphorus. One tissue, two numbers, one protocol.
  4. Self-criticism: they already flagged the known artefacts — the "DNA-P" fraction still contains phosphoprotein (later corrected by the Schneider method or by the Schmidt–Thannhauser–Schneider variants isolating DNA by diphenylamine on the residue). The method succeeded because it did not need to purify anything: it needed only one reaction whose selectivity was already proven by pure chemistry (Levene's alkali observations decades earlier).

Schneider's chain of reasoning (1945):

  1. Drop the alkali, keep the accounting: Schneider accepted that Schmidt–Thannhauser's DNA number was polluted by phosphoprotein. His move was to sidestep phosphorus fractionation entirely: use hot 5% TCA to extract both nucleic acids together out of the protein precipitate — proteins stay coagulated, nucleic acids dissolve — and then measure DNA and RNA in the same extract by their colour reactions: diphenylamine (deoxyribose → blue) for DNA, orcinol (pentose → green) for RNA.
  2. Why hot TCA: cold TCA precipitates macromolecules (the standard "wash" step); boiling TCA, by contrast, depolymerizes and dissolves nucleic acids while proteins denature and stay put — a solubility switch operated purely by temperature, exploiting what precipitation chemists already knew about TCA.
  3. Trade-offs he accepted knowingly: colour reactions are less specific than phosphorus (sugars in the tissue interfere; the orcinol reaction also reads DNA's deoxyribose weakly), so accuracy depends on running standards and blanks. In exchange, both nucleic acids come out in one extract, phosphoprotein no longer contaminates DNA, and any lab with a colorimeter can run it.

Why these two methods became "the" methods (and stayed standard into the 1960s):

  • They fit the instruments of the time — a colorimeter and phosphorus assay, no UV spectrophotometer, ultracentrifuge or electrophoresis needed. A method succeeds historically when it matches what ordinary labs can actually do.
  • They converted a structural difference into a measurand — 2′-OH chemistry (Schmidt–Thannhauser) or acid-solubility at heat (Schneider). Both are instances of the general strategy of this whole era: don't separate the molecules; make one of them change state and measure the two states separately.
  • They were self-correcting — the field cross-validated them against each other and against Bratton's, Ogur–Rosen's and Chargaff's procedures, and each artefact (phosphoprotein contamination, orcinol cross-reaction) had a known correction. The 1950s–60s phenol (Kirby) and detergent (Marmur 1961) methods replaced them only when the question changed from "how much DNA and RNA is in this tissue?" to "give me the intact molecules to sequence, anneal and transform." Schmidt–Thannhauser chemistry — exploiting the 2′-OH — is still the reason RNA is alkali-labile in every modern protocol.
Exam-style summary of the evidence chain:
  1. Chemical: different sugars (ribose vs deoxyribose) and one base difference (uracil vs thymine) — Levene, 1909–1929.
  2. Colour reactions: Feulgen and diphenylamine react only with DNA; orcinol favours RNA-pentose.
  3. Enzymological: specific DNase and RNase (Kunitz crystallization, 1940) — both substrates exist everywhere.
  4. Cytological: Feulgen (DNA in chromosomes), Caspersson UV photometry, Brachet methyl green–pyronine (RNA in nucleolus/cytoplasm).
  5. Biochemical: Schmidt–Thannhauser alkaline split (2′-OH logic), Schneider/Ogur–Rosen extraction, Chargaff's chromatographic base tables.
  6. Functional: only DNA transforms pneumococci (Avery 1944) and enters infected cells (Hershey–Chase 1952).

The Hunt for the Specific Enzymes — Isolation History & Experimental Logic

Every DNA/RNA separation and every nucleotide analysis on this page ultimately depends on enzymes: pepsin to strip protein from nuclei, nucleases to prove the two nucleic acids are different molecules, phosphatases to cut nucleotides apart. Finding and purifying these enzymes was itself a century-long experimental programme with its own methods and its own conceptual battles.

The conceptual problem: what is an enzyme? (1850–1930)

The battlefield. After Pasteur, fermentation was attributed by Liebig's school to "chemical forces" of unstable proteins, while the enzyme (ferment) camp claimed specific, organism-like catalysts. The decisive experiments were isolation experiments:
  • Buchner (1897): cell-free yeast juice (ground with sand and kieselguhr, filtered) still ferments sugar to alcohol and CO₂ → fermentation is chemistry of soluble molecules, not a property of living protoplasm. This founded biochemistry as the chemistry of enzymes, and the sand-grinding + filtration is the ancestor of every cell-free homogenate.
  • Fischer's lock-and-key (1894): glycosidases distinguish α- and β-glycosidic bonds → enzymes have shape specificity, so "a specific enzyme for a specific substrate" became a search warrant: any new class of bond implies a specific enzyme exists to break it.
  • Sumner (1926): crystallizes urease from jack bean (acetone precipitation, chilling) and argues the crystal is the enzyme — a pure protein. Willstätter, the authority of the day, insisted enzymes were not proteins but small active groups adsorbed on colloidal carriers, and his own ultra-purified pepsin gave negative protein tests. Northrop (1930–32): crystallizes pepsin and trypsin, shows constant activity per crystal mass, constant solubility, activity destroyed exactly parallel to protein denaturation, and — decisively — the crystals can be dissolved and recrystallized with the same activity. Willstätter's school had actually purified their enzymes too far: his protein tests were too insensitive for microgram protein. The episode is the classic lesson that absence of a positive test is not absence of the substance.

Enzymes of the nucleic-acid story, in isolation order

Pepsin & trypsin — the protein-removal tools (1836 → 1930)

  • Schwann (1836) names "pepsin" from gastric juice and shows it dissolves meat in acid — the first animal enzyme studied. Miescher's entire nuclein protocol (1869) is built on a pepsin control: the substance resisting pepsin is the discovery criterion.
  • Kühne (1876) coins the word "enzyme" while working on trypsin, and distinguishes "organized ferments" (cells) from "unorganized ferments" (soluble enzymes).
  • Northrop (1930): crystalline pepsin from commercial gastric mucosa: ammonium sulphate fractionation at controlled pH, then ethanol-salt precipitation until a crystalline phase forms. The purified protein was the first enzyme whose kinetics (Michaelis, Northrop's pH optimum work) could be measured without side reactions.

Nucleodepolymerase / DNase (1905 → 1948)

  • Levene & Medigreceanu (1905–06) find in pancreas and other organs an enzyme depolymerizing thymus nucleic acid — they call it "nucleodepolymerase." Assay readout: the viscosity of a DNA solution falls, and the DNA loses its ability to be precipitated/fibrillated — activity long before "units" existed.
  • In the 1920s–40s the enzyme is purified by salt and acetone fractionation (Greenstein, Springer, etc.); named deoxyribonuclease.
  • McCarty (1946, with Avery) crystallizes DNase from pancreas using 0.15 M "acid" ammonium sulphate fractionation — the very enzyme whose specificity had proved the transforming principle was DNA in 1944. Kunitz's route to crystalline DNase followed in 1948–50.
  • Experimental logic of its use: destroy a candidate molecule selectively and ask whether the biological activity dies with it. The 1944 Avery experiment is this logic's most famous execution.

Ribonuclease (1920 → 1940 → 1972)

  • Jones (1920): observing that yeast nucleic acid is degraded in tissue extracts, W. Jones describes heat-stable "nuclease" activity — the first sighting of RNase. Its heat stability (boiling does not destroy it) was itself an isolation clue: it could be separated from most proteins by simply heating the extract.
  • Dubos (1930s) and Brachet's staining work: RNase becomes the specific reagent that removes RNA from tissue sections — the cytological tool of the methyl green–pyronine method.
  • Kunitz (1940): crystalline ribonuclease from beef pancreas. Purification ladder: dilute sulphuric acid extraction → heat to destroy contaminants (RNase survives) → ammonium sulphate and acetone fractionation → crystals. Assay: drop in acid-soluble phosphorus released from RNA, later the Kunitz spectrophotometric assay.
  • Epilogue: RNase A became the first enzyme whose complete amino-acid sequence was determined (Stein, Moore & Hirs, 1960) — closing the loop: Moore and Stein's amino-acid analyzer (1949–51) was applied to the very enzyme family that had made nucleic-acid analysis possible. The 1972 Nobel Prize (Anfinsen, Moore, Stein) is the direct descendant of this enzyme-isolation story.

The discovery narratives in detail — how the DNA-digesting and RNA-digesting enzymes were actually found

Neither nuclease was found by someone looking for "an enzyme that digests DNA/RNA." Both were found because a substrate preparation kept misbehaving — a pattern worth studying as the general way digestive enzymes entered biochemistry.

DNase — found by watching DNA solutions lose their viscosity (1903–1948).

  1. The incidental observation. Around 1903–1905, workers preparing "sodium nucleinate" from thymus noticed that incubating the viscous DNA solution with tissue extracts (pancreas, spleen, liver) made it thin and non-precipitable. Levene and Medigreceanu (1905–06) systematized this at the Rockefeller Institute: they surveyed organs, showed the activity was soluble, heat-labile and destroyed by conditions that denature protein, and named it "nucleodepolymerase." The assay was purely physical: viscosity drop and loss of "fibril" formation on stirring — no chemistry needed, because DNA's huge size made it its own signal.
  2. Why the observation could not be ignored. A 1909-style objection was that the effect was just acid/alkali produced by the extract. The counter-logic: neutral buffered solutions, time-linearity of the viscosity fall, and heat-inactivated controls established that a thermolabile catalyst was acting — the same logic of control experiments as Buchner's fermentation.
  3. The 1930s–40s purification race. Greenstein, Springer and colleagues fractionated the activity with ammonium sulphate and acetone, defined pH optima, and — crucially — established that the pancreatic enzyme acted only on the deoxyribo-type polymer: yeast RNA solutions were untouched. Specificity was the discovery's scientific payload: the enzyme was an analytical reagent proving DNA and RNA are chemically distinct.
  4. Two DNases, not one. In the 1940s an intracellular acid DNase (DNase II) active around pH 5 was distinguished from the pancreatic alkaline DNase (DNase I, pH optimum ~7.5 with Mg²⁺/Ca²⁺ cofactors) — different pH optima, different tissue distributions, different end products. Differential pH optima became the standard way to disentangle "one enzyme or two?" before physical separation was possible.
  5. Crystallization. McCarty (1946) crystallized DNase from pancreas via cold dilute-sulphuric-acid extraction, ammonium sulphate fractionation and controlled acetone precipitation; Kunitz (1948–50) refined the crystallization and converted the viscosity assay into a UV hyperchromicity assay (depolymerized DNA absorbs more at 260 nm) — making DNase measurable kinetically and, incidentally, discovering the hyperchromic effect that became a central probe of DNA structure.
  6. The payoff in the nucleic-acid story. The Avery–MacLeod–McCarty experiment (1944) had used a crude DNase; McCarty's crystallization was undertaken because the 1944 experiment demanded an unquestionably pure, specific DNase. The enzyme found by watching viscosity became the proof that DNA was the transforming principle.

RNase — found by watching RNA preparations fall apart, and by an accident of heat (1920–1940).

  1. The incidental observation. In the 1910s–20s, attempts to prepare yeast nucleic acid from animal tissues (liver, pancreas, spleen) gave degraded, non-precipitable material: the RNA was being digested by the tissue itself. W. Jones (1920) made this explicit with the observation that a boiled pancreas extract still destroys yeast nucleic acid — a shock, because boiling destroys most enzymes. The heat resistance itself became the discovery's fingerprint.
  2. The conceptual move. Jones and later workers treated the destructive activity as a tool rather than a nuisance: if something in tissues digests RNA specifically, then (a) RNA must be a real, chemically distinct substrate in every cell, and (b) the enzyme can be used to remove RNA selectively from mixtures and sections.
  3. Purification exploiting its own property. Dubos (1938) and colleagues purified RNase from beef pancreas using the standard ladder — acid extraction, ammonium sulphate, acetone — plus the step that most enzymes cannot survive: boiling at acid pH to precipitate contaminating proteins while RNase stays active in solution. A quirk of the molecule (its extraordinary stability, later explained by its compact disulphide-bonded structure) became the purification trick.
  4. Kunitz's crystallization (1940) completed the arc: crystals from the heat-treated, salt-fractionated preparation; constant activity per protein mass; a quantitative assay based on release of acid-soluble phosphorus (later the UV assay shared with DNase).
  5. Branching family. Sato and Egami in Japan (1950s) isolated RNase T1 from the mold product "Takadiastase," showing base-specificity (guanine) — opening the era of sequence-specific nucleases (RNase A = pyrimidine-specific). The specificities later became RNA-sequencing tools.
  6. The payoff in the nucleic-acid story. Crystalline RNase was what made Brachet's methyl green–pyronine cytology specific (digest away RNA, the red stain vanishes — so the red material is RNA), what made Hershey–Chase-style isotope work interpretable, and what fixed the Schmidt–Thannhauser alkali method as complementary rather than contradictory (enzyme vs chemistry, two independent routes to the same split).
Did they realize RNase was the reason animal RNA could not be isolated? Yes — and this is one of the great "wrong conclusion from a right diagnosis" episodes in the field. Early workers (Jones, 1920, and even Levene's laboratory) knew that animal-tissue extracts destroyed yeast nucleic acid and suspected a tissue enzyme; Jones explicitly proposed that the difficulty of preparing pentose nucleic acid from animal organs was due to a depolymerizing enzyme. But because the degraded RNA never survived the extraction, the analytical result was simply "animal tissues contain (almost) no RNA" — and this was the textbook dogma through the 1920s (with DNA likewise being reported "absent" from plant tissues, for the mirror-image reason that the isolation chemistry, not the tissue, defined what you could see). The correction came from the 1930s–40s generation — Caspersson's UV photometry, Brachet's nuclease-controlled staining, and Davidson's and Chargaff's biochemical analyses — which showed that animal cells are rich in RNA, concentrated in nucleolus and cytoplasm: the "absence" had been an artefact of RNase attack during grinding and alkaline extraction. The practical lessons written into every protocol since: extract fast, at low temperature, at acid pH or in detergents/phenol that inactivate the enzyme, and add inhibitors — the modern discipline of "RNase-free technique" descends directly from this misdiagnosis corrected.
The general lesson both narratives teach: (1) enzymes were discovered as explanations for contaminated or degrading preparations — the degraded prep is the signal, not the failure; (2) a physical observation (viscosity, solubility, colour) is a sufficient assay if the substrate is distinctive enough; (3) an enzyme's "annoying" properties (RNase's heat stability) become its purification handles; (4) specificity turns an enzyme from an object of study into an analytical instrument — and it was as instruments, not as curiosities, that DNase and RNase settled the DNA/RNA and transforming-principle questions of 1940s biochemistry.

Phosphatases & nucleotidases — dismantling the monomer (1907 → 1940s)

  • Alkaline phosphatase (obstetric enzyme "phosphomonoesterase," described 1907–1911; Grosser & others in bone and placenta) hydrolyses the phosphate ester of nucleotides → became the tool for distinguishing nucleoside from nucleotide (does phosphate release = nucleotide, yes/no?).
  • "Nucleotidase" and "nucleosidase" activities were hunted on the explicit conceptual model: each bond in the nucleotide implies its own specific enzyme (phosphate bond, glycosidic bond). Whether "nucleosidase" was a phosphorolysis or hydrolysis was debated for decades — an early example of discovering that bond-breaking can proceed by more than one chemistry.
  • 5′-nucleotidase (Reis, 1934–37) and the discovery of the nucleotidases in snake venom were crucial for building the nucleotide degradation map — and venom phosphodiesterase became a sequencing tool.

Conceptual guide: how do you find an enzyme that no one has seen?

The five-step experimental programme (as executed 1897–1950):
  1. Invent an assay that converts the invisible into the measurable. A substrate with a physical property (viscosity of DNA; turbidity; acid-soluble phosphorus; gas evolution; colour change; UV absorption) that changes when the enzyme acts. No assay, no enzyme — this is why DNase was "found" as a viscosity drop and RNase as soluble-phosphorus release.
  2. Prove the activity is enzymatic, not chemical. Controls for time-linearity, heat lability, and protein-denaturing reagents; the test-tube must be shown to reproduce in the extract a reaction that is otherwise absent. (Buchner's CO₂; Kunitz's standard curve.)
  3. Ask a specificity question to probe the substrate, not just the enzyme. DNase destroys only DNA → DNA is chemically a distinct molecule; RNase removes only red pyronine stain → the red material is RNA. The enzyme is used as an analytical reagent, and isolation of the substrate (DNA, RNA) proceeds by selective enzymatic destruction.
  4. Purify by fractional precipitation, guided by the assay at every step. Salt (ammonium sulphate) fractionation, pH-controlled precipitation (isoelectric point), organic solvents (ethanol, acetone), heat (exploiting an enzyme's unusual thermostability — the accidental trick behind RNase), adsorption (kaolin, alumina, charcoal), and finally crystallization as the 1930s gold standard of homogeneity.
  5. Prove the crystals ARE the enzyme. Constant activity per mass across recrystallizations; parallel loss of activity and protein on denaturation; testable predictions of kinetics (Michaelis–Menten, 1913, itself formulated while enzymes were still un purified).
Cross-links to the rest of this page: pepsin made Miescher's nuclein discovery possible (1869); DNase proved DNA was the transforming principle (1944); RNase made Brachet's cytology specific (1942) and made the Schmidt–Thannhauser split interpretable; the crystalline enzymes of Kunitz and Northrop were the first proteins sequenced by the chromatographic methods (1949–60) invented for amino acids — meaning the enzyme hunters and the nucleic-acid chemists converged on the same instruments. By the 1950s, "purify until it crystallizes" gave way to "purify until it's sequenceable" — the experimental logic did not change, only the endpoint.

Conceptual Experimental Analysis Guide (1850–1950)

How to think about this century as an exam question or research narrative — five conceptual stages, each with its characteristic experimental logic:

Stage I (1850–1880) — Extraction & elemental analysis. Question: what new substances exist inside cells? Logic: grind, extract with solvents of graded pH, precipitate, then weigh and analyse C/H/N/P. The experiment succeeds when a substance resists known categories (Miescher's pepsin-resistant, phosphorus-rich nuclein). Limitation: mixtures, no structures.
Stage II (1880–1900) — Chemical dissection (Kossel's programme). Question: what is nuclein made of? Logic: hydrolyse the complex, trap each fragment as a crystalline salt (picrates, silver compounds), and match it to synthetic standards (Fischer's purines). Discovery strategy: break down → isolate → identify by comparison. This is where the bases, histones, and "free protein vs nucleic acid" separation belong.
Stage III (1900–1930) — Structure of the monomer & polymer. Question: how are the units connected? Logic: controlled partial hydrolysis defines the monomer (Levene's nucleotide; Fischer's peptide bond); stereochemistry and synthesis prove it. Danger of premature models: Levene's tetranucleotide hypothesis shows how a tidy but wrong structure freezes a field for twenty years — a classic lesson in evidence vs. elegance.
Stage IV (1930–1945) — Enzymes as analytical scalpels. Question: which molecule carries function? Logic: purify to homogeneity, then abolish the activity with a single specific enzyme (DNase in Avery 1944) — the "knockout" logic of pre-molecular biology. Parallel quantitative tools (UV absorption, Kjeldahl nitrogen, color reactions) turn identity claims into measurements.
Stage V (1944–1950s) — Separation science & information. Question: what is the sequence and how is it copied? Logic: chromatography (paper, ion-exchange) makes composition comparable across species; Chargaff's ratios convert analytical chemistry into structural constraint; isotope labelling (³²P/³⁵S in Hershey–Chase) gives molecule-level fate tracking. The conceptual leap: nucleic acids are not just cell chemistry — they are information, and composition rules (A=T, G=C) are the first read of the code.
Recurring experimental motifs to cite in essays:
  • Differential solubility & precipitation (1850s→): the workhorse of every isolation on this page.
  • Hydrolysis as analysis: what strong/weak acids and alkalis reveal about bonds.
  • Derivative trapping (picrates, esters, osazones): making molecules separable by changing their physical form.
  • Enzyme specificity as proof of identity: from Kossel's nucleases to Avery's DNase control.
  • Quantification forcing theory change: spectrophotometry + chromatography killed the tetranucleotide hypothesis.
