How Mu, IS elements and transposons were found

Three kinds of mobile DNA were discovered in E. coli by different routes: a mutagenic phage, mutations that would not revert, and drug resistance that moved between plasmids. Each result turned out to describe the same process, transposition.

The three discoveries

Where each element came from

All three are DNA segments that move to new sites without needing sequence homology and without the host's RecA recombination system.

Phage Mu

A virus that mutates its host

Larry Taylor (UC Berkeley) noticed in the late 1950s that infection by phage Mu-1 produced mutations at many different loci in E. coli. He named it Mu for "mutator" and compared it to McClintock's controlling elements in maize (PNAS, 1963). Its genome is 36,717 nucleotides.

IS elements

Mutations that would not revert normally

Strongly polar mutations in the gal and lac operons did not behave like point mutations, frameshifts or deletions. Shapiro in London and Jordan, Saedler and Starlinger in Cologne showed by the end of 1968 that they were insertions of DNA pieces. They were named insertion sequences (IS).

Tn elements

Resistance genes that jump

Antibiotic resistance spread between plasmids even in recA mutant hosts. Hedges and Jacob (1974) called these units of transposition "transposons". Tn3 carries ampicillin resistance. Tn5 and Tn10 are composite: a resistance gene flanked by two IS copies.

    Experiments

    Four experiments that defined the concept

    What was done, what was seen, and what it proved.

    1. Mu-induced mutations at many loci (Taylor, 1963)

    Done

    E. coli K-12 was infected with Mu-1 and lysogens were screened for new nutritional requirements and other mutant phenotypes.

    Seen

    Mutations appeared at many different genes, and the phage was the cause. Later work found a prophage sitting inside the mutated gene.

    Showed

    A phage genome can integrate into many sites and disrupt the gene it lands in. Bukhari and Zipser (1972) tested this at a single gene and found Mu DNA inserted at many positions within it. Electron microscopy (Martuscelli et al., 1971) showed Mu inserted as a linear piece in lysogens.

    2. Polar gal and lac mutations are insertions (1967 to 1972)

    Done

    Spontaneous gal mutations were selected for failure to grow on galactose. They were mapped, tested for reversion and suppression, and the DNA from reverting and non-reverting strains was compared by density and by electron microscopy.

    Seen

    The mutations were strongly polar, shutting off genes downstream in the operon. Suppressors and mutagens that act on point mutations did not affect them. Reversion by recombination or loss changed the DNA's buoyant density, consistent with foreign DNA being present. Heteroduplex electron microscopy then located the insertions, and lac and gal insertions turned out to be a small set of specific sequences.

    Showed

    Short, specific DNA segments (IS elements) insert into genes, silence downstream genes and can be lost again. Multiple copies of IS1 and IS2 exist in E. coli K-12 and on F and R plasmids.

    3. Ampicillin resistance moves without RecA (1974 to 1979)

    Done

    Ampicillin resistance was followed between plasmids in conjugation crosses, in hosts with and without the recA gene. The moved segment was then mapped by heteroduplex electron microscopy, and Tn3 was later sequenced.

    Seen

    The resistance gene moved equally well in recA strains, so homologous recombination was not needed. Tn3 is 4,957 bp with 38 bp inverted repeats. Besides the bla gene it encodes a transposase and a resolvase. Insertion leaves a 5 bp direct repeat.

    Showed

    Genes can ride on a transposable unit. Composite elements such as Tn10 (about 9.3 kb, flanked by IS10) and Tn5 (flanked by IS50) are two IS copies carrying a gene between them, and the IS copies can also move on their own.

    4. Replicative transposition and cointegrates (1979 to 1985)

    Done

    Cointegrate structures (two replicons fused with two copies of the element) were analysed for Mu and Tn3. Mizuuchi (1983) then reproduced Mu transposition in vitro with purified components.

    Seen

    Mu replication is tied to transposition, and the element stays at its donor site while a copy arrives at a new one. Craigie and Mizuuchi (1985) isolated the strand-transfer intermediate in which donor and target DNA are covalently joined.

    Showed

    Shapiro's 1979 model: element ends join the target through staggered cuts, creating replication forks at each end. Replication through the element gives the cointegrate, and resolution gives one copy at each site. Tn10 and Tn5 behave differently from Tn3: Tn5 transposition does not depend on recA, while its cointegrate formation does.
