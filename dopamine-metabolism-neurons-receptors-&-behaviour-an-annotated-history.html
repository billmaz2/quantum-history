1 · A brief history

  • 1910–11 — Barger & Dale survey sympathomimetic amines and note that dopamine is the decarboxylated precursor of noradrenaline without itself being strongly pressor; Casimir Funk synthesizes L-DOPA the following year. Nobody yet suspects a transmitter role. [1]
  • 1957–58 — Montagu detects dopamine in animal tissues, and Carlsson, Lindqvist, Magnusson & Walbeck show it is present in brain — not merely a noradrenaline precursor but a transmitter candidate in its own right; reserpine sedation is reversed by L-DOPA. [2], [3]
  • 1959–60 — Bertler & Rosengren find dopamine concentrated in the corpus striatum and propose its role in Parkinson's disease; Ehringer & Hornykiewicz demonstrate the striatal dopamine deficit in post-mortem parkinsonian brains — the founding lesion of transmitter-based neurology. [4], [5]
  • 1961–67 — Birkmayer & Hornykiewicz report the first L-DOPA infusion in parkinsonian patients, and Cotzias establishes high-dose oral L-DOPA as effective chronic therapy — the birth of modern neurotransmitter-replacement medicine. [6], [7]
  • 1963–64 — Carlsson & Lindqvist show that antipsychotics increase dopamine turnover — the first pharmacological hint of antipsychotic action at dopamine synapses; Dahlström & Fuxe map the central catecholamine neurons into cell groups A1–A15, with the dopamine groups A8, A9, A10 (and hypothalamic A11–A15) defined. [8]
  • 1968–71 — Ungerstedt introduces the 6-hydroxydopamine striatal lesion and the rotation model — experimental parkinsonism in the rat. [9], [10]
  • 1972–75 — Kebabian, Petzold & Greengard discover the dopamine-sensitive adenylate cyclase, and radioligand binding (Creese, Burt & Snyder) makes dopamine receptors directly measurable. [11], [12]
  • 1979 — Kebabian & Calne formalize the two-receptor classification, D1 (adenylate-cyclase-linked) and D2 (cyclase-independent) — the scheme that still organizes the field. [13]
  • 1983 — Langston, Ballard and colleagues trace a cluster of young parkinsonian drug users to the meperidine-analog contaminant MPTP, which selectively destroys nigrostriatal dopamine neurons — accidental human proof of the dopamine hypothesis of Parkinson's disease. [14]
  • 1988–91 — The molecular era: Bunzow and colleagues clone the D2 receptor; Sokoloff the D3; Van Tol the D4 (revealing clozapine's affinity and the DRD4 polymorphism); Dearry the D1; Sunahara the D5; and Shimada the cocaine-sensitive dopamine transporter DAT. [15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20]
  • 1986–2001 — PET imaging (Wong, Farde, Kapur & Seeman) establishes that clinical antipsychotic effect requires ~65–75% striatal D2 receptor occupancy — the quantitative anchor of the dopamine hypothesis of schizophrenia. [21], [22]
  • 1989–97 — The reward literature: Wise & Rompre's dopamine-and-reward synthesis, and Schultz's single-neuron recordings formalizing dopamine neurons as reward-prediction error signals — the substrate now invoked in addiction, motivation and reinforcement-learning theory. [23], [24]
  • 1995–97 — Gene × behaviour: the DRD4 exon-III repeat polymorphism is associated with the human personality trait of novelty seeking; Giros' dopamine-transporter knockout mice are hyperactive and indifferent to cocaine and amphetamine — defining DAT as the shared site of psychostimulant action; Zetterström's Nurr1-null mice lose their midbrain dopamine neurons. [25], [26], [27]
  • 2000s — Transcription-factor genetics (Pitx3, Lmx1b) mark the substantia nigra sublineage; human imaging ties striatal dopamine to prediction-error signalling and addiction. [28]

2 · Metabolism and the enzymes of the pathway

Dopamine is synthesised from the essential amino acid L-tyrosine in two steps, stored by VMAT2, inactivated by oxidative deamination and O-methylation, and cleared from the synapse by the plasma-membrane transporter DAT. In noradrenergic neurons it is also the immediate precursor of noradrenaline:

Enzymes

Tyrosine hydroxylase (TH)

The rate-limiting, BH4 (tetrahydrobiopterin)-dependent hydroxylation of tyrosine to L-DOPA — the committed step of all catecholamine synthesis. TH is controlled by phosphorylation at serine residues (Ser40 by PKA) and by end-product feedback inhibition by catecholamines bound at the iron centre. Dominant TH mutations cause L-DOPA-responsive dystonia (Segawa disease), and recessive loss causes infantile parkinsonism. [11]

Aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase (AADC/DDC)

A pyridoxal-5′-phosphate-dependent enzyme that decarboxylates L-DOPA to dopamine (and 5-HTP to serotonin); shared between catecholamine and indoleamine pathways and not rate-limiting. This is why peripheral L-DOPA is co-administered with carbidopa (an AADC inhibitor that does not cross the blood–brain barrier) — concentrating conversion inside the brain while preventing nausea. Inherited AADC deficiency causes a combined monoamine neurotransmitter disorder.

Monoamine oxidase A & B (MAO-A/B) + COMT

The principal catabolic route: oxidative deamination of dopamine to DOPAL, aldehyde dehydrogenase to DOPAC, then O-methylation by catechol-O-methyltransferase to HVA — the CSF and urinary turnover index. MAO-B inhibitors (selegiline, rasagiline) and COMT inhibitors (entacapone, tolcapone) extend each L-DOPA dose in Parkinson's disease. COMT is also the enzyme methylating catechol estrogens and detoxifying dietary catechols.

Dopamine β-hydroxylase (DBH)

The vesicular ascorbate-dependent enzyme that converts dopamine to noradrenaline inside secretory vesicles — the metabolic boundary between a dopaminergic and a noradrenergic neuron. Human DBH deficiency produces severe orthostatic hypotension; conversely, dopamine persists as an end-product in neurons that lack DBH.

Note: unlike serotonin (mostly peripheral), the body's dopamine is dominated by the brain pools — nigrostriatal (~80% of brain DA), mesolimbic/mesocortical and hypothalamic — with peripheral roles in the kidney (natriuresis), the retina and the endocrine prolactin axis. Cytoplasmic dopamine auto-oxidizes to neuromelanin, which gives the substantia nigra its name.

3 · Anatomy: the dopaminergic cell groups A8–A15

All forebrain dopamine arises from a small set of midline neurons defined by Dahlström & Fuxe (1964) with the Falck–Hillarp histofluorescence method [8]. The modern account divides them into three long-axoned systems (nigrostriatal, mesolimbic, mesocortical) plus short hypothalamic and intraneuronal (retina, olfactory bulb) populations.

Physiology: midbrain dopamine neurons fire slowly (~1–5 Hz) in pacemaker or low-frequency bursts; burst firing and the phasic "signal" depend on NMDA-receptor-driven plateau potentials, and are powerfully regulated by D2 somatodendritic autoreceptors (a several-second delay before firing is silenced by D2 agonists). Grace's tonically active population (~50%) and transiently active "reserve" populations form the framework linking tonic and phasic dopamine signalling. Schultz's recordings showed these neurons encode a reward-prediction error — firing to unexpected rewards and to cues predicting them, and depressing when predicted rewards are omitted [24].

4 · Receptor subtypes and behavioural correlates

All dopamine receptors are G-protein-coupled (rhodopsin-like). The founding D1/D2 pharmacological classification of Kebabian & Calne [13] survives as the D1-like (D1, D5, Gs-coupled) and D2-like (D2, D3, D4, Gi/o-coupled) families, with molecular cloning in 1988–91 [15], [18].

Transporters complete the picture: DAT (SLC6A3) clears synaptic dopamine — the shared binding site of cocaine and methylphenidate, and of amphetamine's releasing action. DAT knockout mice are hyperactive and, remarkably, indifferent to cocaine and amphetamine [26] — the clearest single-gene proof of the transporter hypothesis of psychostimulant action. DAT density imaged by 18F-DOPA and DAT-SPECT is the standard biomarker of nigrostriatal degeneration in parkinsonian syndromes.

5 · Lesion and depletion studies

Pharmacological depletion

  • Reserpine — the vesicular monoamine (VMAT2) blocker that depletes stored dopamine, serotonin and noradrenaline. Carlsson showed that reserpinized animals become akinetic and immobile but recover when given L-DOPA — the pivotal 1957 experiment establishing dopamine as a brain transmitter and leading directly to the L-DOPA therapy of Parkinson's disease [3]. Reserpine was also the first antipsychotic used clinically, foreshadowing the D2 hypothesis.
  • α-Methyl-p-tyrosine (AMPT) — a tyrosine hydroxylase inhibitor that depletes dopamine synthesis; in humans it transiently decreases reward responsiveness and can precipitate depressive symptoms, tying catecholamine synthesis to mood and motivation.
  • AMPT / acute phenylalanine-tyrosine depletion — the standard experimental method for reducing human brain dopamine during PET or pharmacological-challenge studies of depression and addiction.
  • MPTP — the meperidine-analog contaminant (Langston's heroin-using patients, 1983) that selectively destroys nigrostriatal dopamine neurons and produces a near-pure parkinsonian syndrome in humans and primates; MPTP-treated primates remain the standard preclinical model of Parkinson's disease [14].

Neurotoxic and genetic lesions

  • 6-Hydroxydopamine (6-OHDA) — a DAT/NET-taken-up neurotoxin that selectively destroys catecholamine neurons. Ungerstedt's unilateral striatal 6-OHDA lesion with apomorphine-induced rotation (1968–71) [9], [10] remains the workhorse model for testing antiparkinsonian and dyskinesia drugs; protection by desipramine established pathway specificity versus the noradrenergic system.
  • Electrolytic/excitotoxic lesions of the VTA and substantia nigra — reduce accumbal and striatal dopamine and blunt psychostimulant self-administration and conditioned place preference; nucleus accumbens terminal lesions defined the mesolimbic substrate of drug reward.
  • Genetic "lesions" of the phenotype — Nurr1 (nuclear receptor) is required for the generation and maintenance of midbrain dopamine neurons: Nurr1-null mice are born without a dopamine phenotype and die neonatally [27]; Pitx3 (paired-like homeobox) is required selectively for substantia-nigra (A9) survival — Pitx3-deficient mice lose nigral but not VTA neurons, mirroring Parkinson's disease specificity [28]; and DAT knockout mice (Giros) show the expected hyperdopaminergic, hyperlocomotor, cocaine-insensitive phenotype [26].
  • Human genetic deficits — recessive tyrosine hydroxylase deficiency and aromatic-amino-acid decarboxylase deficiency both present as L-DOPA(-responsive) dystonia-parkinsonism of infancy, proving that intact dopamine synthesis is required for normal human motor development. Segawa disease (dominant TH mutation with diurnal fluctuation) responds dramatically to low-dose L-DOPA.
