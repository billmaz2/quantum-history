From Quantum Mechanics to Quantum Electrodynamics

A historical timeline (1900–1950) tracing how our understanding of light and matter evolved — from Planck's desperate "act of despair" to Feynman diagrams and the most precisely tested theory in science. Each entry separates the conceptual leaps from the experimental evidence that forced them.

Era I · The Quantum Is Born (1900–1912)

Classical physics said hot objects should radiate infinite energy. They don't. Fixing that single problem planted the seed of everything that follows.

1900

Planck's Quantum of Action

Concept

Max Planck — Berlin

To derive the spectrum of light from a hot object (blackbody radiation), Planck assumed that energy is exchanged between matter and light only in discrete packets: E = nhν. He called the mathematics a lucky guess; the constant h was introduced as a bookkeeping device.

Detailed example — Ultraviolet catastrophe Classical physics (Rayleigh–Jeans law) predicts that a warm oven at temperature T emits spectral energy density u(ν) = 8πkTν²/c³, which grows without bound as frequency increases — implying every object should instantly blaze with ultraviolet and X-rays. Planck's formula u(ν) = 8πhν³/c³ · 1/(e^{hν/kT} − 1) instead falls off exponentially at high ν, because each quantum costs hν and high-energy quanta become exponentially rare. Planck fitted the data with h ≈ 6.55×10⁻³⁴ J·s — within 1% of today's value.
1905

Einstein's Light Quanta (the Photoelectric Effect)

Concept + Experiment

Albert Einstein — Bern, Swiss Patent Office

Going far beyond Planck, Einstein proposed that light itself is granular — not just absorbed in chunks. This "heuristic point of view" explained why light can kick electrons out of metal.

Detailed example — Millikan's verdict (1916) Robert Millikan set out to refute Einstein and instead confirmed him precisely. He measured the maximum kinetic energy of photo-ejected electrons: K_max = hν − φ, where φ is the metal's work function. Three classical impossibilities are explained at once: (1) no electrons below a threshold frequency, no matter the intensity — because each quantum carries fixed energy hν; (2) electron energy depends on frequency, not brightness — brighter light means more quanta, not bigger quanta; (3) emission is instantaneous (<10⁻⁹ s) — no time needed to "accumulate" wave energy. Millikan's slope gave h = 6.57×10⁻³⁴ J·s, matching blackbody data. (Einstein's Nobel Prize, 1921, was for this — not relativity.)
1911

The Nuclear Atom

Experiment

Ernest Rutherford, Geiger, Marsden — Manchester

Alpha particles fired at gold foil occasionally bounced straight back — impossible for J.J. Thomson's "plum pudding" atom. The atom's positive charge must be concentrated in a tiny, dense nucleus.

Detailed example — The backscattering surprise About 1 in 8,000 alphas deflected by more than 90°. Rutherford: "as if you fired a 15-inch shell at tissue paper and it came back." Coulomb scattering gives the impact parameter b = (k·Z₁Z₂e² / 2K)·cot(θ/2); for θ = 180°, b ≈ 10⁻¹⁴ m, forcing the nuclear radius below ~10⁻¹⁴ m — 10,000× smaller than the atom itself. This created a crisis: an orbiting electron is an accelerating charge, which classically radiates and should spiral into the nucleus in ~10⁻¹¹ seconds. Atoms should not exist.

Era II · The Old Quantum Theory (1913–1922)

Quantum rules are bolted onto classical orbits. It works brilliantly for hydrogen — and fails for everything else, showing something deeper is needed.

1913

Bohr's Atomic Model

Concept + Experiment

Niels Bohr — Manchester / Copenhagen

Bohr declared that electrons occupy only certain "stationary" orbits, and light is emitted only when they jump between them: hν = E₂ − E₁. Radiation became a quantum jump between discrete states.

Detailed example — The hydrogen spectrum, quantitatively Bohr's energies: Eₙ = −13.6 eV / n². The jump from n=3 to n=2 (Balmer Hα line): ν = R(1/2² − 1/3²) with R = 1.097×10⁷ m⁻¹, predicting λ = 656.3 nm — matching the observed red line of hydrogen to the accuracy of spectroscopy itself. Even better: 1885 Balmer series, 1908 Paschen (infrared), and the model predicted the Lyman ultraviolet series (discovered 1906–14) and Brackett series correctly — predictions made before some lines were measured.
Where it broke The helium atom defeated Bohr's model completely: predicted spectra wrong by ~4%. Worse, the fine structure of spectral lines (splitting seen with good spectroscopes, explained by Sommerfeld in 1915 using relativistic ellipses) and the anomalous Zeeman effect (patterning of lines in magnetic fields) obeyed rules that no orbit model could rationalize. By the early 1920s the old quantum theory was a patchwork of ad-hoc rules.
1922–24

The Compton Effect & Particle–Wave Duality

Concept + Experiment

Arthur Compton (experiment) · Louis de Broglie (concept)

Compton showed X-rays scattering off electrons shift in wavelength exactly as if they were particles obeying billiard-ball conservation laws. Then de Broglie inverted the logic: if waves act like particles, particles must act like waves — λ = h/p.

Detailed example — Compton scattering kinematics Treating the X-ray photon as a particle of energy E = hc/λ and momentum p = h/λ, energy and momentum conservation give the wavelength shift: Δλ = (h/mₑc)(1 − cos θ), with the "Compton wavelength" h/mₑc = 2.43×10⁻¹² m. At θ = 90°, the shift is 2.43 pm — independent of the incoming wavelength, exactly as observed. Skeptics (including Bohr, who had briefly abandoned light quanta) conceded the photon was real. De Broglie's inverse hypothesis was confirmed in 1927 when Davisson and Germer saw electrons diffract off nickel crystals with the predicted λ = h/mv — electrons behaving as waves.

Era III · Quantum Mechanics Proper (1923–1927)

Within four dizzying years, two complete mathematical frameworks appear, are proven equivalent, and the uncertainty principle reshapes what "knowing" means.

1925

Matrix Mechanics — the first quantum mechanics

Concept

Werner Heisenberg, Max Born, Pascual Jordan — Göttingen · Niels Bohr — Copenhagen

Heisenberg's radical move: keep only what is measurable. His paper was titled "On a quantum-theoretical reinterpretation of kinematic and mechanical relations." Position and momentum became non-commuting objects — later recognized as matrices — and classical orbits were abandoned entirely.

Detailed example — The commutation relation Born and Jordan formalized: pq − qp = (h/2πi)·1 — i.e. [x, p] = iħ. Consequence: states with definite position cannot have definite momentum. Applied to the harmonic oscillator, the energies come out quantized automatically: Eₙ = ħω(n + ½) — including a surprising irreducible ground-state energy ½ħω, the zero-point energy, later confirmed by the still-liquid behavior of helium at absolute zero.
1926

Schrödinger's Wave Equation

Concept + Experiment

Erwin Schrödinger — Zurich · Max Born — Göttingen (probabilistic reading)

Guided by de Broglie's matter waves, Schrödinger wrote a differential equation for them. The hydrogen problem's solutions reproduced Bohr's energies — now as standing-wave eigenstates, no orbits needed. Born then supplied the meaning: the wave's square is a probability.

Detailed example — The hydrogen ground state as a standing wave Solving Hψ = Eψ with a Coulomb potential, normalizable solutions exist only for Eₙ = −mₑe⁴/(2ħ²n²) = −13.6 eV/n² — Bohr's formula, but now derived, not postulated. Schrödinger's equation also explains chemical bonding: the same equation applied to the H₂⁺ molecular ion shows the electron's wavefunction can lower its kinetic energy by spreading over two protons, giving a stable bond — the birth of quantum chemistry. Within months, Pauli (and independently Dirac, Eckart) proved matrix and wave mechanics are the same theory.
1927

Uncertainty, Exclusion, and the Copenhagen Framework

Concept

Heisenberg (uncertainty) · Wolfgang Pauli (exclusion, 1925) · Bohr (complementarity)

Detailed example — Uncertainty in the microscope Heisenberg's original thought experiment: to locate an electron you must illuminate it with a photon, but the photon's recoil disturbs its momentum. The relation Δx·Δp ≥ ħ/2 is not a measurement flaw — it is a property of the non-commuting operators. Pauli's exclusion principle (no two electrons in the same quantum state) explains the entire periodic table: shells fill up, chemistry exists, and neutron stars avoid collapse.

Era IV · From Particles to Fields — the Dirac Era (1927–1932)

Quantum mechanics can't handle photons — you can create and destroy them. Fixing that means quantizing the electromagnetic field itself. QED is born.

1927

Dirac Quantizes the Electromagnetic Field

Concept

P.A.M. Dirac — Cambridge

The founding paper of QED. Dirac treated each mode of the radiation field as a quantum harmonic oscillator, with creation and annihilation operators for photons. Spontaneous emission — Bohr's mysterious quantum jumps — emerged automatically.

Detailed example — Why atoms emit "spontaneously" Every mode of the vacuum still holds zero-point fluctuations ½ħω. Even in the lowest state, the vacuum field jiggles the atom's electron, stimulating the transition 2p → 1s. Dirac's calculation of Einstein's A coefficient: A₂₁ = (4/3)·(ω³|d|²)/(4πε₀ħc³) — matching measured hydrogen lifetimes (~1.6 ns for the 2p state). Spontaneous emission is emission stimulated by the vacuum.
1928

The Dirac Equation — Relativity Meets Quantum Mechanics

Concept + Experiment

P.A.M. Dirac — Cambridge

Dirac linearized E² = p²c² + m²c⁴ in the energy to make Schrödinger's equation Lorentz-invariant. Spin-½ and the correct magnetic moment fell out for free. But the equation had twice as many solutions as needed — including negative-energy states.

Detailed example — Three predictions, one equation (1) Electron spin: the Dirac equation yields g = 2 for the electron's magnetic moment — previously a fudge. (2) Fine structure: Dirac's hydrogen levels, including relativistic and spin–orbit effects, exactly explained Sommerfeld's fine-structure splittings. (3) Antimatter: the "holes" in the negative-energy sea were predicted in 1931 as a positive electron — Carl Anderson photographed the positron in cosmic-ray tracks in August 1932. Prediction: year 1931. Discovery: year 1932. Experiment vindicated the strangest feature of the theory.
1929–30

Infinities Appear Immediately

Concept (a problem!)

Oppenheimer, Waller, Weisskopf, Dirac, Heisenberg, Pauli

Applying the new QED to self-energy — an electron interacting with the field it itself generates — produced logarithmically divergent (infinite) corrections to the electron's mass and charge. These infinities stalled the field for nearly two decades.

Detailed example — The divergent self-energy Summing the vacuum fluctuation energy over all modes of the electron's own Coulomb field gives δm ~ (e²/4πε₀)·∫ d³k/k² → ∞, a divergence that is only logarithmic in Dirac's theory (Weisskopf, 1934) but fatal all the same. Opinions of the era: Dirac wanted to abandon QED and start over; Bohr and Rosenfeld argued the infinities might dissolve with a finite measurement apparatus. Nobody yet realized the answer was not a new theory but a new bookkeeping of the old one.

Era V · The Long Stall & the Crisis Years (1930s)

Two seemingly unrelated experiments — on radar vacuum tubes and hydrogen spectroscopy — end up resolving the infinities.

1932–1940s

Regularization, Hole Theory, and Honest Despair

Concept

Weisskopf, Pauli & Heisenberg, Kramers, Bethe, Fermi

Progress shifted to nuclear physics (more tractable), while a small community kept refining QED's failure modes. Kramers, in his 1937–38 lectures, seeded the crucial idea: perhaps the observed mass and charge must be inserted into the theory, making the bare ones irrelevant — the core of what would become renormalization.

Detailed example — Rabi's beam method (1930s) In parallel, I.I. Rabi's molecular-beam magnetic-resonance method (1938) reached relative precision ~10⁻⁴ in measuring magnetic moments — far beyond older Stern–Gerlach techniques. His student Willis Lamb and Polykarp Kush would use wartime radar technology to make this precision 100× better. The crisis of 1947 was technologically possible only because radar had been invented.
1946

The Electron's Magnetic Moment Shows a Crack

Experiment

Nafe, Nelson & Rabi — Columbia

Precision microwave spectroscopy of hydrogen and deuterium hyperfine splitting suggested the electron's g-factor was not exactly 2 as Dirac demanded, but slightly larger. The hint was ~0.1% off and the community was skeptical — but it spurred Lamb to build his definitive experiment.

Why g = 2 + ε matters The magnetic moment: μ = g·(e/2m)·S. Dirac: g = 2 exactly. Any deviation means the electron couples to vacuum fluctuations — direct evidence that the "empty" field must be treated as dynamical, i.e. QED must be a field theory, not a single-particle wave mechanics.

Era VI · Shelter Island, Renormalization & the Triumph of QED (1947–1950)

Four days in June 1947 changed physics. The experiments reported there broke Dirac's theory, and within three years QED was reborn as the most accurate theory ever built.

1947

The Lamb Shift — the Experiment That Broke Dirac's Atom

Experiment

Willis Lamb & Robert Retherford — Columbia

Using surplus radar technology, Lamb and Retherford drove transitions between hydrogen's 2S and 2P levels with microwaves. Dirac's theory says these are exactly degenerate. They are not.

Detailed example — Numbers that ended an era Lamb and Retherford measured a splitting of 2S₁/₂ − 2P₁/₂ ≈ 1000 MHz (≈ 4 μeV — a whisper, ~5 parts in 10⁷ of the level's binding energy). Reported at the Shelter Island Conference (June 2–4, 1947) alongside Kush's refined g-factor: g = 2 × 1.00119 ± 3. Both measurements contradicted the otherwise-perfect Dirac equation. Bethe famously did the calculation on the train home from Shelter Island.
1947–49

Renormalization — Subtracting the Infinities

Concept

Bethe (non-relativistic seed, 1947) · Sin-Itiro Tomonaga (Tokyo) · Julian Schwinger (Harvard) · Richard Feynman (Cornell)

The idea that saved QED: infinite quantum corrections to mass and charge are unobservable because we only ever measure the physical, dressed values. Absorb the infinities into a finite, measured mass and charge — and the remaining predictions are finite, unique, and testable.

Detailed example — Bethe's train calculation (June 1947) Treating the atom non-relativistically and cutting off the divergent integral at mₑc² ("the electron can't absorb a photon more energetic than its own rest energy"), Bethe computed the Lamb shift: ΔE ≈ 1040 MHz — the measured 1000 MHz, to 4%. The cutoff-dependent infinite part could be dumped into the definition of mass. The finite residue was physics.

g-factor (Schwinger, 1948): g = 2·(1 + α/2π) = 2 × 1.001161…

anomalous magnetic moment (aₑ = (g−2)/2):
aₑ = α/(2π) − 0.328478965…(α/π)² + 1.181241…(α/π)³ − …

Lamb shift (leading log): ΔE ≈ (4α⁵mₑc²/3π)·ln(1/α) ≈ 1000 MHz
1948–49

Three Proofs, One Theory — Tomonaga, Schwinger, Feynman

Concept

Tomonaga (war-isolated Tokyo, 1943–48) · Schwinger (operator method, 1948) · Feynman (path integrals & diagrams, 1948) · unified by Dyson, 1949

Tomonaga had developed a fully relativistic, covariant QED during the war, unknown in the West until 1947. Schwinger built a formidable operator formalism; Feynman an intuitive diagrammatic one from his path integrals. Freeman Dyson proved in 1949 that the two approaches — and Tomonaga's — were mathematically equivalent.

Detailed example — Reading a Feynman diagram
Each diagram is a term in a series in α = e²/4πħc ≈ 1/137. Each internal loop contributes a divergent integral — but Dyson showed the divergences always cluster into just two quantities, the electron's mass and charge, at every order. Two experimental inputs, mₑ and e, buy infinite predictions. This systematic absorbability is renormalizability.
Detailed example — The g-factor showdown, 1948 At the Pocono Conference (March–April 1948), Schwinger announced aₑ = α/2π = 0.00116. The 1947 experiment had given 0.00119 ± 0.00003 — the first confirmation that vacuum fluctuations have measurable, calculable effects. Precision has since climbed: today aₑ(exp) = 1 159 652 180.73(0.28)×10⁻¹² while QED through tenth order agrees to within a few parts per trillion — QED is the most accurately verified theory in the history of science, and α remains the only free parameter standing between experiment and prediction.

Legacy · What QED Started

The arc: a bookkeeping trick (1900) → light as particles (1905) → quantized orbits (1913) → matter as waves (1923–26) → fields as oscillators (1927) → antimatter (1928) → infinities (1930) → radar-driven precision experiments (1947) → renormalization (1948) → the most accurate theory ever (today). Each step was forced by an experiment that the previous theory could not explain.
1950s →

The Template for All Modern Field Theory

Concept

The renormalization toolkit built for QED became the pattern for everything after it: Yang–Mills gauge theory (1954), the electroweak theory (1961–68, photon reappears as part of a unified field with W and Z), QCD (1973, "the QED of the strong force" with color charge), and the Standard Model (1970s). Feynman's Nobel lecture put it simply: the theory of quantum electrodynamics is "the jewel of physics — our proudest possession."

Conceptual take-aways to remember (1) Photons are the quanta of the field itself — not particles traveling through space, but excitation levels of oscillators that fill all space. (2) Particles can be created and destroyed — this is why quantum mechanics alone was insufficient. (3) Vacuum is not empty — Lamb shift, anomalous moment, and Casimir force all probe the vacuum's zero-point content. (4) Infinities are not failures — renormalization says that some infinities live in unobservable parameters and must be measured, not computed. (5) Agreement with experiment is the standard — QED survives because its predictions work to one part in a trillion.
