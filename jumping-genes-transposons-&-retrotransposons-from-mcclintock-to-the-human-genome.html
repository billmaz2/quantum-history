Jumping Genes: Transposons & Retrotransposons

From Barbara McClintock's spotted maize kernels to the discovery of Alu, the selfish-DNA debate, LINE-1 disease insertions, and the 2001 Human Genome Project revelation that our DNA is roughly half transposon-derived. Every claim sourced with a working DOI.

1 · McClintock and the origin of the idea (1944–1950)

The concept of mobile genes was born decades before molecular biology could explain it — from the careful observation of broken chromosomes and variegated corn kernels.

Barbara McClintock, at Cold Spring Harbor, was studying the behavior of broken chromosomes in maize. In 1944–45, following up plants descended from a gamete that had received a broken chromosome, she noticed a chromosome that seemed to lose and regain a piece of itself — instability that couldn't be explained by any known mechanism. By tracking this "ring chromosome" through mitoses, she deduced that a chromosomal segment was being gained and lost during development.

That led her to the famous Dissociation (Ds) locus: a site on chromosome 9 where chromosomes broke — but only when a second, unlinked genetic element, Activator (Ac), was present in the same plant. Ds did not sit still: with Ac, it could move — into or near genes — and when it left again, gene function was restored. A kernel whose cells gained and lost Ds during development showed mosaic spots of pigment: her "controlling elements" were turning genes on and off and moving around the genome.

"The conclusion seems inescapable that the transposition of chromosomal units provides a mechanism for control of gene action… In the light of present knowledge, these considerations force us to re-examine our concepts of the gene and the genome." — McClintock, on her controlling elements (1950)

She reported the work at the 1951 Cold Spring Harbor Symposium and in her 1950 PNAS paper, The Origin and Behavior of Mutable Loci in Maize (doi:10.1073/pnas.36.6.344). The reception was cold silence: the genome was widely seen as a fixed string of genes, and her results seemed to violate that dogma. She largely stopped publishing on the system for years — and kept working on it for three decades.

✅ Why it mattered: McClintock had discovered (1) mobile genetic elements, (2) a two-component regulatory system (autonomous Ac controlling non-autonomous Ds), and (3) insertional mutation and its precise or nearly precise excision — all in one body of purely genetic work. Vindication came from bacteria (Section 3), and she received the unshared Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1983, over thirty years later.

2 · The mystery of repeated DNA (1968–1971)

While McClintock worked alone, biochemists were stumbling into a different mystery: most eukaryotic DNA was repetitive — and nobody knew what it was for.

The C₀t experiment Roy Britten and David Kohne measured how fast sheared, denatured DNA strands from various species renature (re-find their partners). Mouse and human DNA renatured in fast and slow fractions — meaning hundreds of thousands of copies of certain sequences were scattered through the genome, vastly more than genes could explain. Some repeats were short and interspersed; others were long. Their 1968 Science review, Repeated Sequences in DNA, made the problem unavoidable (doi:10.1126/science.161.3841.529).

Britten and Eric Davidson (1969, Science 165:349–357) proposed that repetitive DNA served gene regulation — "sensor" and "integrator" repeated sequences networking the genome. This adaptive view dominated until 1980, when the selfish-DNA papers (Section 6) reframed much of the same data. Both views were partly right, and the resolution would only come when the repeats were identified as transposable elements.

3 · Bacteria make it respectable again: insertion sequences and transposons (1967–1975)

The rediscovery of mobile DNA came from bacterial genetics — and this time it could be pinned to physical DNA.

In the late 1960s, several groups studied "extreme polar" mutations in E. coli operons — mutations that not only knocked out one gene but shut off every gene downstream (the same polarity phenomenon Jacob and Monod had used to argue that operons are transcribed as units). The surprise: these mutations were caused not by base changes but by the physical insertion of extra DNA.

Density-gradient proof (Shapiro, 1969) James Shapiro isolated λ-transducing phages carrying either a wild-type galactose operon or a spontaneous extreme-polar gal mutation, and compared their buoyant density in CsCl gradients. The mutant phage DNA was heavier — it contained more DNA. Reverting or recombining the mutation away removed the extra DNA. The simplest explanation: a discrete segment of DNA had been linearly inserted into the operon. (J. Mol. Biol. 40:93–105, doi:10.1016/0022-2836(69)90298-8.) Starlinger and Saedler's group reached the same conclusion for gal, and characterized the inserted segments.

Within a few years the field exploded:

  • The bacterial mobile segments were named Insertion Sequences (IS elements) — small (~700–1,500 bp) units flanked by inverted repeats, encoding a transposase that cuts the element and pastes it elsewhere ("cut-and-paste" transposition), duplicating a short target sequence at the insertion site.
  • Composite transposons such as Tn10 (tetracycline resistance) and complex ones like Tn3 (ampicillin resistance) were discovered — two IS elements flanking resistance genes, carrying them across plasmids and chromosomes. Antibiotic resistance spread through bacterial populations because of transposition.
  • Transposons showed a distinctive signature — target-site duplications flanking each copy — a fingerprint that later allowed bioinformaticians to identify fossilized transposons across eukaryotic genomes, including ours.
✅ Why it mattered: bacterial genetics gave mobility a physical mechanism — real DNA segments, real enzymes, real recombination chemistry. Once "jumping genes" were molecules rather than McClintock's genetic abstractions, her maize results were reread as the same phenomenon. She was awarded the Nobel Prize in 1983, a year after mobile elements had been fully molecularly mainstream.

4 · Reverse transcriptase: the enzyme that reverses the Central Dogma (1970)

Retroviruses had long suggested genes could move — and in 1970 the enzyme that makes it possible was found simultaneously by two labs.

Howard Temin had proposed since 1964 that RNA tumor viruses replicate through a DNA intermediate (the "provirus" hypothesis) — the viral RNA being copied into DNA and integrated into the host chromosome, exactly what we now call retrotransposition. The idea was widely disbelieved, since it violated the Central Dogma's DNA → RNA → protein direction.

Two papers, one issue of Nature (June 1970) Temin & Mizutani, working with Rous sarcoma virus, and David Baltimore, working with Rauscher mouse leukemia virus, independently found a RNA-dependent DNA polymerase inside purified virions — an enzyme that copies RNA into DNA: reverse transcriptase. (Temin & Mizutani, doi:10.1038/2261211a0; Baltimore, doi:10.1038/2261209a0. Nobel Prize 1975, shared with Renato Dulbecco.)
✅ Why it mattered: reverse transcriptase was the missing engine for a second, entirely different mode of transposition — copy-and-paste via RNA. It explained retroviruses, and it predicted that genomes might contain viral-like elements that copy themselves through RNA intermediates. It also became the single most important tool of the molecular biology era: cDNA synthesis, cloning, reverse-transcription PCR, and eventually retrotransposition assays.

5 · Retrotransposons: proving the "copy-and-paste" life cycle (1980–1985)

Eukaryotes from yeast to flies to humans turned out to be full of elements that transpose through RNA — and the Ty experiments proved it beautifully.

By the late 1970s, eukaryotic repetitive DNA was being sorted into families with telltale transposon-like features: flanking target-site duplications, poly(A)-like tails, and in some cases LTRs (long terminal repeats) looking exactly like miniaturized retroviruses — copia in Drosophila, Ty in yeast. Genome sequence databases were not yet available, but hybridization and early sequencing suggested these elements moved like retroviruses.

The intron trick (Boeke, Garfinkel, Styles & Fink, 1985) Jef Boeke and Gerald Fink engineered a Ty1 element of Saccharomyces cerevisiae placed under a controllable GAL1 promoter, and marked it by inserting a fragment containing an intron inside the Ty sequence. When the marked element transposed to new genomic sites, the intron had been spliced out of the new copies. Only a transcript that passed through the nucleus and had its intron removed by RNA splicing — and was then reverse-transcribed back into DNA — could leave that signature. Retrotransposons transpose through an RNA intermediate (Cell 40:491–500, doi:10.1016/0092-8674(85)90197-7). Boeke's group soon reconstituted Ty retrotransposition in vitro and named the replication intermediates "VLP-like" particles — the element packages its own RNA and reverse transcriptase, like a domesticated retrovirus.

In contrast, McClintock's Ac/Ds and bacterial transposons are DNA transposons — they move as DNA (cut-and-paste), with no RNA step.

6 · The selfish-DNA controversy (1980): what are all these copies for?

With transposons and repetitive DNA both confirmed, one question became unavoidable: why do genomes tolerate so much of it? Two back-to-back Nature papers gave the unsettling answer: maybe for nothing at all.

"The majority of self-replicating DNA… can be regarded as 'selfish' DNA, a piece of DNA with no phenotypic function whose only property is that it spreads within the genome." — paraphrasing Orgel & Crick (1980)

W. Ford Doolittle and Carmen Sapienza (Nature 284:601–603, doi:10.1038/284601a0) and, in the same issue, Leslie Orgel and Francis Crick (Nature 284:604–607, doi:10.1038/284604a0) argued that natural selection acts within genomes as well as between organisms: a DNA sequence that is good at copying itself will accumulate copies, whether or not that benefits the host. Transposable elements, middle-repetitive DNA and pseudogene-like copies could be genomic parasites with no phenotype at all.

The debate was fierce — "junk DNA" became a slogan partly from this moment. The modern resolution is more nuanced than either camp: most individual transposon copies are indeed dead fossils and drift to decay, but the process of transposition has been a massive creative force — supplying new regulatory sequences, new exons, genomic rearrangement hotspots, and even evolutionary innovations (a story largely visible only after 2001, and continuing today).

7 · Alu and the SINEs: the genome's most successful parasite (1979–1985)

The most abundant repeat family in the human genome began as a band on a restriction digest — named after the restriction enzyme that revealed it.

Finding a 300-nucleotide repeat everywhere (Jelinek et al., 1979–80) Carl Schmid, Prescott Deininger, Catherine Houck, Warren Jelinek and colleagues showed that a ~300-bp sequence was interspersed throughout mammalian genomes — present roughly 300,000–500,000 (later measured at over 1,000,000) times in human DNA, scattered between genes and inside introns. A related fragment had earlier been seen as a 300-bp band released by the restriction enzyme AluI — hence the family's name. The key papers: Houck et al., J. Mol. Biol. 132:289 (1979), and Jelinek, Schmid et al., PNAS 77:1398–1402 (doi:10.1073/pnas.77.3.1398), which unified the DNA family, the abundant small RNAs found hydrogen-bonded in hnRNA, and RNA polymerase III transcripts as one and the same family.

What Alu turned out to be

  • A dimer: two similar ~130-bp halves separated by an A-rich linker, ending in a poly(A)-like tail — the signature of retrotransposition (retrotransposed copies are made from RNA, hence the poly(A) tail baked into the DNA copy).
  • A fossil of 7SL RNA: Elisabetta Ullu and Christine Tschudi showed Alu is a processed copy of 7SL RNA — the signal-recognition-particle RNA — with the central 7SL-specific segment deleted (Nature 312:171–172, doi:10.1038/312171a0). Each Alu carries an internal RNA polymerase III promoter, which is why it can be transcribed at all.
  • Non-autonomous: Alu encodes no protein. It is a passenger that retrotransposes by borrowing the machinery of LINE-1 — Alu RNA is recruited into L1 ribonucleoprotein particles and reverse-transcribed by L1's ORF2 protein. This is the defining feature of SINEs (Short INterspersed Elements) generally: tRNA- or 7SL-derived non-coding elements parasitic on LINE-encoded enzymology.
  • Subfamilies and "master genes": Deininger, Schmid and colleagues showed Alu copies fall into subfamilies of different ages, amplified in waves from a small number of hyperactive "master" source genes (J. Mol. Evol. 29:504–512, 1989). Different Alu subfamilies date to different primate radiations — making Alu insertions superb markers of primate phylogeny (they insert uniquely, are essentially never lost, and have a known polarity).
✅ By 1985 the picture was complete: the human genome contained two enormous, cooperating retrotransposing systems — autonomous LINE-1 elements that encode the enzymatic machinery, and a million-odd Alu SINEs riding on it — plus tens of thousands of dead LTR retrotransposons and DNA transposon fossils. Together they would turn out to account for a staggering share of our DNA, a fact that no one could quantify until the genome was actually sequenced.

8 · Retrotransposition strikes in living humans: L1 and haemophilia (1988)

Selfish DNA was still an abstraction until it was caught causing a human disease — in a patient, in that generation.

A de novo insertion (Kazazian et al., 1988) Haig Kazazian's group screened 240 unrelated patients with haemophilia A for defects in the factor VIII gene. In two patients, they found that a LINE-1 element had inserted de novo into exon 14 of the factor VIII gene — the insertion was absent from the parents' genomes. This was a somatic, ongoing retrotransposition event: an L1 element in that family's genome had transposed into a gene and caused disease (Nature 332:164–166, doi:10.1038/332164a0).

Human L1 is not a fossil: a small number of active L1Ta elements (with intact ORF1 and ORF2) still jump, at roughly one new retrotransposition per ~20–100 births — and, through the L1 machinery, they mobilize Alu and SVA insertions too. Mobile element insertions are now a recognized class of human mutation, responsible for tens of documented cases of disease (including haemophilia, Duchenne muscular dystrophy, neurofibromatosis, and cancer-driving somatic insertions).

9 · The Human Genome, 2001: the genome as a transposon graveyard (and workshop)

In February 2001, two rival efforts published the human genome sequence — and delivered the final verdict on half a century of transposon biology.

The public Human Genome Sequencing Consortium (Lander et al., Nature 409:860–921, doi:10.1038/35057062) and Celera's whole-genome shotgun effort (Venter et al., Science 291:1304–1351, doi:10.1126/science.1058040) each devoted a major section of their analysis to transposable elements, identified by their signature sequences and target-site duplications across the 3-billion-base sequence.

Headline numbers from the draft human genome

~45%of human DNA is derived from transposable elements
~21%LINEs (mostly LINE-1)
~13%SINEs (Alu ≈ 10%)
~8% retrotransposons (endogenous retroviruses)
~3%DNA transposons (all extinct in humans)
1.5%or less — protein-coding genes

Striking conclusions of the 2001 analyses included:

  • The genome is mostly older than the genome. The elements are overwhelmingly ancient and decayed: two whole classes of DNA transposon had already gone extinct in the human lineage, and even the "live" LINE-1 elements are mostly 5′-truncated, inactive copies. A given interspersed repeat is on average ~12–18% diverged from its family consensus.
  • A "two-mode" landscape. Regions of high gene density were enriched for Alu (and Alu remnants appear to have contributed regulatory motifs to genes), while gene-poor deserts accumulated LINE-1 — the retroelements effectively documented their own insertion preferences across the genome.
  • Protein-coding exons are made of former transposons. In dozens of genes, transposable-element-derived sequences had been domesticated into exons (of the ~1,000 human genes whose coding regions contain recognizable TE-derived protein sequence, fragments of transposase and reverse transcriptase domains appear in real human proteins).
  • Centromeres and other "unsequenceable" regions were estimated to be massively transposon-rich.
  • Both papers noted that the count was a floor, not a ceiling: elements too old to recognize were invisible to homology searches. The 2001 estimate of ~45% (with only ~5% in coding sequence) has only grown with improved repeat annotation of the "finished" and telomere-to-telomere assemblies. The story that started with variegated maize kernels ended with the finding that transposons are, by mass, the largest component of the human genome.
