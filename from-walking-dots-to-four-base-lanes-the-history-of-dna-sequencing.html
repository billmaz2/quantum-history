From Walking Dots to Four Base Lanes

The history of DNA sequencing runs straight through protein chemistry and RNA sequencing: Sanger's insulin fingerprints, Edman's stepwise degradation, Holley's tRNA, the two-dimensional "fingerprint" maps and wandering spots of ³²P-labelled oligonucleotides, the plus-and-minus method, Maxam–Gilbert chemistry — and finally the four-lane dideoxy gel that read DNA like a printed sentence.

1 · Where "sequence" was invented: proteins first (1943–1958)

The very idea that a biological polymer has a unique, exact, readout-able sequence of monomers was proved on a protein — not on DNA.

In the 1940s it was not obvious that proteins were definite chemicals at all: many believed they were statistical mixtures. Frederick Sanger, in Cambridge, destroyed that idea with insulin. His strategy became the template for every sequencing method since:

  • Label one end. Sanger used fluorodinitrobenzene (FDNB) to tag the free amino groups of a protein, so the N-terminal amino acid of every fragment could be identified by its yellow colour.
  • Cut the polymer into small pieces — by partial acid hydrolysis, and more cleanly by proteolytic enzymes (trypsin, pepsin).
  • Separate the fragments two-dimensionally — by paper chromatography and electrophoresis, producing the spot patterns Sanger called "fingerprints."
  • Overlap the fragments. Fragments from different digests share parts of their sequences; overlapping them lets you reconstruct the whole.

The B chain ("phenylalanyl chain") fell in 1951 (Sanger & Tuppy, Biochem. J. 49:463, doi:10.1042/bj0490463 and 49:481, doi:10.1042/bj0490481), the A chain by 1953, and the disulfide bonds in 1955 (Ryle et al., Biochem. J. 60:541, doi:10.1042/bj0600541). Insulin — 51 amino acids — had one exact sequence. Proteins were molecules. Sanger won his first Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1958 for this work.

Edman's rival approach: strip residues one at a time (1950) Pehr Edman in Sweden took a different route: phenyl isothiocyanate (PITC) couples to the N-terminal residue under mild acid, cleaving only that one amino acid as a phenylthiohydantoin, leaving the rest of the chain intact — so the same peptide can be degraded repeatedly, reading one residue per cycle (Acta Chem. Scand. 4:283–293, 1950; doi:10.3891/acta.chem.scand.04-0283). Automated as the "sequenator" (Edman & Begg 1967), this dominated protein sequencing for decades — and its logic (stepwise, one-residue-per-cycle) prefigures the chain-terminating logic Sanger later used for DNA.
✅ What sequencing inherited from proteins: (1) the strategy of fragment, separate in two dimensions, and overlap; (2) the idea of end-labeling; (3) the idea of stepwise degradation; and (4) Sanger himself — who now turned the same way of thinking toward nucleic acids.

2 · RNA sequencing: fingerprints, homochromatography, and "wandering spots" (1965–1969)

Before DNA could be sequenced, RNA could — because RNA comes in small pieces, can be made radioactive, and can be cut at predictable bases with enzymes.

Holley's tRNA — the first nucleic acid ever sequenced (1965) Robert Holley's team at Cornell spent seven years on yeast alanine tRNA (77 nucleotides). They cut the RNA into fragments with pancreatic and T1 ribonucleases (which cleave after U and G, respectively), purified the oligonucleotide fragments, sequenced each small fragment, and overlapped them — exactly Sanger's insulin logic transplanted to RNA (Science 147:1462–1465, doi:10.1126/science.147.3664.1462). Nobel Prize 1968, shared with Khorana and Nirenberg.
Sanger's radioactive fingerprinting for RNA (1965–67) Sanger, George Brownlee and Bart Barrell replaced chemical fragment identification with radiolabelling: grow cells in ³²P so every nucleotide in the RNA is radioactive; digest with RNases; then separate the labeled oligonucleotides in two dimensions — electrophoresis in one direction, "homochromatography" (displacement chromatography on DEAE-paper or thin layers) in the other. Each fragment is a spot on an autoradiograph (J. Mol. Biol. 13:373–398, doi:10.1016/S0022-2836(65)80104-8). Because T1 digestion gives fragments ending in G, the sequence of each spot can be worked out by further digestion and by mobility-shift analysis — the "wandering spot" method, in which a spot is digested one nucleotide at a time and its walk across the 2D map (each removed base shifts the spot in a characteristic, base-specific direction) is read as sequence. The 120 nucleotides of E. coli 5S rRNA followed in 1967 (Brownlee, Sanger & Barrell, J. Mol. Biol. 23:337–353, doi:10.1016/S0022-2836(67)80109-8), then tRNAs fell rapidly, and by 1969 fragments of phage R17 RNA were being read.
This is the "walking dots" era in its pure form: sequence information lived in the positions and movements of radioactive spots on two-dimensional maps, deciphered by hand, one oligonucleotide at a time.
✅ Why RNA sequencing mattered for DNA: it created the tool kit — ³²P end-labelling, base-specific cleavage, and the analysis of families of fragments differing by single bases — and it created the appetite. RNA had 4 letters and small molecules; DNA had 4 letters and huge molecules. The obvious next question was how to read DNA itself.

3 · The first true DNA sequences (1968–1973)

DNA was harder: no convenient base-specific enzymes, enormous molecules, no way to label it at one defined point. The first DNA sequences were wrestled out a few nucleotides at a time.

Ray Wu and the λ cohesive ends (1968–1971) Ray Wu exploited a natural gift: bacteriophage λ DNA has single-stranded 12-nucleotide "cohesive ends" — a known starting point. By adding one or a few radioactive nucleotides to the 3′ ends with DNA polymerase and analyzing the products enzymatically, Wu's lab determined the first real DNA sequences: the 12-nucleotide cohesive ends of λ (J. Mol. Biol. 51:501–521, doi:10.1016/0022-2836(70)90004-5) and their completion (J. Mol. Biol. 57:491–511, doi:10.1016/0022-2836(71)90105-7). Twelve bases — a decade after insulin's 51 amino acids.

In 1973 Walter Gilbert and Allan Maxam took a different route to their 27-base lac operator sequence (see the lac operon story): they used the purified lac repressor to protect the operator DNA from DNase, recovered the protected fragment, transcribed it into RNA in vitro, and sequenced that RNA by Sanger's fingerprint/wandering-spot methods (PNAS 70:3581–3584, doi:10.1073/pnas.70.12.3581). Sanger's group likewise read early DNA by copying it into RNA-like substrates — including 50 nucleotides of phage f1 DNA in 1973 using homochromatography and wandering-spot analysis. DNA was being sequenced through the RNA methods, a dozen bases at a time. Something radically faster was needed.

4 · The "walking dots" of DNA sequencing: plus and minus (1975)

Sanger's first DNA method read sequence from the characteristic wander of radioactive spots on homochromatography plates — dots that walked in base-specific directions.

Plus and minus (Sanger & Coulson, 1975) The insight: DNA polymerase can copy DNA from a primer, and you can control which base the copying stops at. An oligonucleotide primer is annealed to the single-stranded DNA; polymerase extends it in four separate reactions, each missing one dNTP. In the "minus" reaction for, say, A, synthesis stops when the template demands an A — so every product ends exactly one base before an A. The "plus" reactions use the peculiar ability of polymerase to add one extra base and stall, marking positions of that base. The four products are separated on a 2D system (electrophoresis + homochromatography) and read as an autoradiograph of spots: as the products get longer, each successive spot walks in a direction characteristic of the next base, and the sequence is read from the geometry of the walk (J. Mol. Biol. 94:441–448, doi:10.1016/0022-2836(75)90213-2).

The plus-and-minus method was genuinely rapid for its day — and Sanger's lab used it to read the entire 5,375-nucleotide genome of bacteriophage φX174, the first complete DNA genome ever sequenced (Nature 265:687–695, doi:10.1038/265687a0). The paper revealed overlapping genes and stunned the field. But the method was error-prone (30 corrections were later needed), hard to read, and limited to specialized single-stranded templates and natural priming sites. The walking dots had reached their limit.

5 · 1977: two independent revolutions

In one year, two laboratories on two sides of the Atlantic replaced spot-reading with something radically simpler: read positions directly off a one-dimensional ladder of fragments.

Maxam–Gilbert: chemical sequencing (Harvard) End-label the DNA with ³²P at one end. Then run four tubes of chemistry that cleave the DNA partially and preferentially at one base: dimethyl sulfate (G, and A under acid), hydrazine (C, and C+T with salt omitted). Each tube becomes a nested family of labeled fragments whose lengths record every position of that base. Separate all four on a polyacrylamide gel and read the ladder (PNAS 74:560–564, doi:10.1073/pnas.74.2.560). No enzymes, no primer, any double-stranded DNA. For a decade this was the method of choice for regulatory regions — Gilbert used it on the lac promoter/operator (see the operon page), and it earned a share of the 1980 Nobel Prize.
Sanger: chain termination with ddNTPs (Cambridge) Sanger's genius was to reuse the plus-and-minus geometry but make the chain stops chemically permanent and perfectly random. He added the 2′,3′-dideoxy analogues of the normal triphosphates to each polymerase reaction: when a ddNTP is incorporated instead of a normal dNTP, the chain lacks the 3′-OH and cannot be extended. One tube per base, each containing a mixture of normal and dideoxy nucleotide, generates a nested ladder of fragments, every one ending exactly at that base. Read the four ladders side by side on a gel, and the sequence reads off bottom-to-top like a sentence (PNAS 74:5463–5467, doi:10.1073/pnas.74.12.5463).
✅ What both methods shared: the "ladder" principle — convert a sequence into four nested families of fragments, one per base, differing by one unit of length, and let the gel do the arithmetic. Position on the gel = position in the sequence. The two-dimensional wandering spot was dead; the one-dimensional ladder of four base lanes had arrived.

6 · Reading the four lanes (1977–1983)

The familiar four-lane autoradiograph — A, C, G, T — is the visual form of the dideoxy method, polished over the following five years.

  • Thin denaturing polyacrylamide gels (1978): Sanger & Coulson showed that very thin gels (FEBS Lett. 87:107–110, doi:10.1016/0014-5793(78)80145-8) resolve single-base differences far better than the earlier systems — hundreds of readable bases per gel, with the classic four black bands per lane read bottom-up. One person could run dozens of ladders a week.
  • Sequencing by shotgun cloning: the M13 vector gave clean single-stranded templates with a universal primer site, so any DNA — not just phage genomes — could be cloned at random, sequenced, and reassembled by overlaps. Sanger's group used "shotgun + dideoxy" to finish the 48,502-base human mitochondrial genome in 1981, then bacteriophage λ (1982), and drew the roadmap that would eventually be scaled to the Human Genome Project.
  • Readable technology: ³²P labeling, then ³⁵S for sharper bands; manual film development; and human eyes reading "GATC" off four lanes — the iconic image of the 1980s–90s sequencing lab.

The ladder that ended the walking-dot era. Each lane contains one dideoxynucleotide; the shortest fragments run fastest (lowest); the sequence is the order of the lanes hit, read from the bottom of the gel upward.

7 · From four lanes to the Human Genome Project (1983–2001)

The dideoxy chemistry never changed after 1977 — everything that followed was engineering around it.

  • Colored terminators (1987): four fluorescent dyes attached to the ddNTPs themselves, letting all four reactions run in one lane or one capillary, with a laser and detector reading the bases as they pass — the end of film development and of the four-lane autoradiograph, but the direct descendant of it.
  • Thermal cycling & automation: cycle sequencing with thermostable polymerase, robotic handling, base-calling software. The Applied Biosystems fluorescence machines of the 1990s read hundreds of bases per reaction with no radioactivity.
  • Whole-genome strategies: Sanger's shotgun-plus-overlap strategy, scaled to BAC clones (public HGP) or entire genomes (Venter's Celera), both published in February 2001 (Lander et al., Nature 409:860, doi:10.1038/35057062; Venter et al., Science 291:1304, doi:10.1126/science.1058040). Maxam–Gilbert chemistry faded away; every base of the first human genome was read by chain termination — Sanger's 1977 reaction, a billion-fold scale-up.
  • What came next: pyrosequencing (454), Illumina sequencing-by-synthesis, nanopores — all are conceptually variations on the ladder principle: turn each base into an event you can count or see at a position.
