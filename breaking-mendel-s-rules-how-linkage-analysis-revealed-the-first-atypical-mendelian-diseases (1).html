Breaking Mendel's Rules

How linkage analysis and positional cloning uncovered the first "atypical" Mendelian diseases — disorders whose inheritance could not be explained by simple point mutations passed from parent to child.

Between 1980 and 1997, human geneticists did something once thought impossible: they found disease genes without knowing what the gene did. The method — linkage analysis — traced which DNA markers travel with a disease through families, mapped the gene to a chromosome, then walked the DNA to find the culprit. When the dust settled, six kinds of mutation emerged that nobody had predicted.

What is linkage analysis, and why did it matter?

The breakthrough began with Botstein, White, Skolnick & Davis (1980), who proposed that inherited restriction fragment length polymorphisms (RFLPs) could act as signposts across the human genome. If a disease and an RFLP are co-inherited in families more often than chance predicts (a high LOD score), the disease gene must sit near that marker. This turned genetics from "study the protein" into "follow the DNA" — a strategy later called positional cloning.

The first great triumph was Huntington's disease, linked to chromosome 4 in 1983 — even though the gene itself took another 10 years. In the years that followed, positional cloning revealed disease mechanisms that violated the textbook Mendelian rules: genes could be switched off by a parent's sex, could grow longer from generation to generation, could be duplicated wholesale, could jump into place from elsewhere in the genome, or could appear in a child with no family history at all.

1Mechanism: mutation deep inside an intron

Friedreich's Ataxia — the disease gene in the "junk" middle of a gene (1996)

Classical Mendelian genetics assumed mutations change the protein-coding part of a gene. Friedreich's ataxia — a fatal autosomal-recessive neurodegenerative disease described by Nikolaus Friedreich in 1863 — shattered that assumption. Families were linked to chromosome 9 in 1988, but for eight years no coding mutation could be found in any candidate gene.

The process: Chamberlain, Shaw & colleagues first mapped the disease to chromosome 9q13-q21 by linkage in 1988. After years of positional cloning by the Pandolfo/Koenig consortium, the answer emerged in 1996: the gene (FXN, encoding frataxin) was intact in most patients — but hidden in intron 1 lay an expanded GAA trinucleotide repeat (up to >1,000 copies vs. the normal ~8–33). The intronic expansion silences the gene by forming unusual DNA structures that block transcription. It was the first common disease shown to be caused by a mutation entirely outside the coding sequence — proof that introns, far from junk, could hold the key to a disease.

2Mechanism: genomic imprinting — the parent's sex matters

Prader-Willi & Angelman Syndromes — when the same DNA is silent or active depending on who gave it (1981–1997)

Mendel's laws say a gene is a gene, whichever parent transmits it. Prader-Willi syndrome (PWS) and Angelman syndrome (AS) proved otherwise. In 1981, Ledbetter and colleagues showed PWS was caused by a deletion of chromosome 15q11-q13 — but curiously, children with a seemingly identical deletion could instead have Angelman syndrome, a completely different disease.

The process: Linkage and molecular studies narrowed the critical region. In 1989 Nicholls, Knoll, Butler, Karam & Lalande reported non-deletion PWS patients with maternal heterodisomy — two copies of chromosome 15, both from mother. That could only cause disease if genes in the region were imprinted: silenced on the maternal copy and expressed only from the paternal copy (and vice versa for AS). Thus "same deletion, opposite disease" was explained: PWS loses the paternal expression, AS loses the maternal one. In 1997 Kishino, Lalande & Wagstaff identified UBE3A (a ubiquitin ligase) as the maternally expressed gene mutated in Angelman syndrome — one of the first disorders defined as an imprinting defect, a non-Mendelian mechanism in which epigenetic marks, not DNA sequence, decide the disease.

3Mechanism: copy number variation — too many copies of a healthy gene

Charcot-Marie-Tooth Disease Type 1A (CMT1A) — a disease caused by an extra copy, not a broken gene (1989–1992)

Dominant diseases were assumed to be caused by mutations that damage a protein. CMT1A, an inherited peripheral neuropathy, broke that rule. In 1989 linkage mapped CMT1A to chromosome 17p11.2-p12. Then in 1991 Lupski's group, studying CMT families — including one that also had neurofibromatosis type 1, allowing a chromosomal "jump" strategy from NF1 — discovered that most patients carried a 1.5 Mb tandem duplication of 17p12. No base was changed; the gene itself was normal.

The process: positional cloning across the duplicated region showed it contained the PMP22 gene. In 1992 Lupski's team demonstrated that gene dosage — three copies of a perfectly good PMP22 gene — is the disease mechanism; the reciprocal deletion of the same interval causes the milder neuropathy HNPP (hereditary neuropathy with liability to pressure palsies), producing a fascinating "opposite" phenotype. This was the first inherited disease shown to result from copy number variation — an idea that would explode into the CNV era of the 2000s.

4Mechanism: dynamic trinucleotide repeat expansion — a mutation that grows

Fragile X, Kennedy Disease, Myotonic Dystrophy & Huntington's — mutations that expand between generations (1969–1993)

Mendelian mutations were assumed stable: parents pass them, children inherit them, unchanged. The discovery of unstable trinucleotide repeats overturned this. Fragile X syndrome — the most common inherited intellectual disability — had puzzled geneticists since Herbert Lubs described the "marker X chromosome" in 1969. Families showed the baffling Sherman paradox: risk and severity increased across generations, and unaffected "transmitting males" existed — patterns impossible under classical Mendelian rules.

The process: Linkage analysis placed the fragile X locus at Xq27.3. In 1991 three landmark papers converged: Kremer/Richards mapped the instability to a (CCG)n repeat; Verkerk isolated the FMR1 gene; Fu resolved the Sherman paradox by showing the repeat expands from a "premutation" (~55–200 copies) to a full mutation (>200 copies), which silences the gene by methylation. The same year, La Spada & Fischbeck showed Kennedy's disease (X-linked spinal-bulbar muscular atrophy) is caused by CAG expansion in the androgen receptor — the first repeat in a coding region. Brook's group showed myotonic dystrophy was a CTG expansion in 1992, and in 1993 the Huntington's Disease Collaborative Research Group closed the decade-long hunt that began with Gusella's 1983 chromosome 4 linkage: a CAG expansion in the HTT gene. These "dynamic mutations" explained anticipation — a phenomenon dismissed for decades as observational bias — and showed DNA itself can be structurally unstable and mutate its own length.

5Mechanism: transposon insertion — "jumping genes" cause disease

Hemophilia A — LINE-1 retrotransposons jumping into Factor VIII (1988)

Barbara McClintock had discovered transposable elements in maize in the 1940s (Nobel Prize 1983), but few believed "jumping genes" mattered in human disease. In 1988, Kazazian, Wong, Youssoufian, Scott, Phillips & Antonarakis reported two boys with severe hemophilia A whose factor VIII gene had been disrupted in their germline de novo by the insertion of a LINE-1 (L1) retrotransposon — a mobile element copied from elsewhere in the genome and pasted into the gene.

The backstory — why Kazazian was looking at these children's genes at all: Kazazian was not hunting for transposons — he and his Johns Hopkins colleague Stylianos Antonarakis were running one of the first systematic mutation surveys of a fully established disease gene. Hemophilia A was the perfect model for this: the F8 gene had been cloned in 1984 by the Genentech group (Wood, Gitschier, Lawn and colleagues), and unlike most diseases of the era, hemophilia A offered everything a mutation-hunter needed — a precisely understood protein, an easily measurable clinical severity, abundant patient samples from hemophilia treatment centers, and, crucially, a famously high rate of new mutation: roughly one third of boys with hemophilia A were the first affected member of their family. That last fact was the scientific magnet. Every "sporadic" case represented a fresh germline mutation that had arisen — in a parent's germ cells or in the embryo — and Kazazian's group wanted to know what kinds of mutations the human genome actually makes. So they screened the factor VIII gene of large numbers of patients with Southern blots and other molecular tools, cataloguing point mutations, CpG transitions, and deletions. Among the boys with no family history — precisely the sporadic cases they were most interested in — two showed something that fit no known category: the gene was not deleted, not point-mutated, but disrupted by a large unknown insertion. The discovery was serendipity meeting preparedness: in the same laboratory sat Alan F. Scott, one of the world's experts on the human L1 (LINE-1) repeat family. When the inserted DNA was isolated and sequenced, it was instantly recognizable — a copy of a human L1 retrotransposon, transposed de novo into the factor VIII gene of each child, in one case with flanking target-site duplications, the molecular signature of retrotransposition. In other words: in these two "sporadic" children, the new mutation the team had been searching for was not a copying error at all — it was a jumping gene. McClintock's maize transposons, brought into human medicine.

The follow-up: The Nature paper of March 1988 demonstrated the phenomenon; the group then pursued the source. Dombroski, Mathias, Nanthakumar, Scott & Kazazis/Kazazian isolated the actual progenitor element — an active, full-length L1 (later named L1.2, on chromosome 22) capable of retrotransposition — published in Science in 1991. Meanwhile Sklower Brod, Kazazian and colleagues (Am J Hum Genet, 1988) had characterized a further L1 insertion family. Together these papers proved that human L1 elements are not inert genomic fossils but still-mobile, disease-causing mutagens in the germline — the mechanism Nature's title called "a novel mechanism for mutation in man." Today insertions of L1, Alu and SVA elements are recognized causes of dozens of inherited and somatic diseases, and the very mutational category Kazazian stumbled upon in sporadic hemophilia is now studied genome-wide.

Why this mattered for genetics: the finding showed that a "well-established" Mendelian disease with a fully cloned gene still held a completely unpredicted mutation mechanism — and that new-mutation (sporadic) cases are the richest place to look for novel mechanisms, because whatever broke the gene in those boys had happened de novo, in living memory, and could be caught in the act.

6Mechanism: de novo (non-parental) mutation — no family history at all

Achondroplasia — the most common dwarfism is almost never inherited (1994)

Classical linkage analysis requires multigenerational families — a de novo mutation, present in a child but in neither parent, seemed to fall outside its reach. Achondroplasia solved this puzzle. Although one of the oldest recognized conditions (depicted in ancient Egyptian art), it appears in about 1 in 25,000 births, and over 80% of patients have unaffected parents — the mutation arises fresh, overwhelmingly in the father's sperm, with risk increasing with paternal age.

The process: In 1994, linkage studies in affected families mapped achondroplasia to chromosome 4p16.3 (with further fine-mapping of the interval). Within months, two groups showed that virtually all cases are caused by the same G380R mutation in FGFR3 (fibroblast growth factor receptor 3) — a G-to-A transition at a CpG hotspot that arises repeatedly and independently, explaining both familial cases (autosomal dominant) and the many sporadic ones. It became the textbook example of a high de novo mutation rate at a specific nucleotide, and of the link between advanced paternal age and new dominant mutations. Achondroplasia showed that a "genetic disease" can strike without any family history — a recurring mutation, not an inherited one.
