DNA Superhelicity

An educational timeline: how supercoiled DNA was discovered, how the topoisomerases that control it were found, and how gel electrophoresis came to separate the topoisomers — individual species that differ by ΔLk = ±1.

1 · The core idea in one minute

A covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA) has no free ends, so the number of times its two strands wind around each other is locked in. That integer is the linking number, Lk. A relaxed B-DNA helix "wants" one turn per ~10.5 base pairs; if Lk differs from that relaxed value, the circle cannot unwind at the ends — instead it writhes, coiling up on itself like a twisted rubber band. That coiled state is superhelical DNA.

Lk = Tw + Wr   ·   σ = (Lk − Lk₀) / Lk₀   ·   ΔLk = ±1 between adjacent topoisomers

Lk = linking number (a topological invariant), Tw = twist (helical winding), Wr = writhe (supercoils). σ is the superhelix density; natural DNAs are typically negatively supercoiled, σ ≈ −0.05 to −0.07.

2 · Timeline of discovery

1963 discovery

Animal-virus DNA turns out to be a closed circle

Dulbecco and Vogt show that polyoma virus DNA sediments in two forms. The faster form is a ring: a covalently closed duplex circle — the substrate on which all supercoiling physics would later be worked out.

Dulbecco & Vogt, Evidence for a ring structure of polyoma virus DNA, PNAS 50:236 (1963) · DOI 10.1073/pnas.50.2.236

1965 discovery

The "twisted circular form" — superhelical DNA is described

Vinograd, Lebowitz, Radloff, Watson & Laipis report that closed circular polyoma DNA exists in a compact, rapidly sedimenting twisted form, and that a single-strand nick converts it to the relaxed open circle. This is the birth of DNA superhelicity as an experimental fact: the twist is topologically trapped because the strands cannot rotate freely.

Vinograd et al., The twisted circular form of polyoma viral DNA, PNAS 53:1104 (1965) · PubMed 4287964 · DOI 10.1073/pnas.53.6.1104

1967 method

Dye–buoyant density: supercoiled DNA is purified in a centrifuge

Radloff, Bauer & Vinograd exploit the fact that closed circles bind less ethidium bromide than nicked DNA, so they band at a different density in CsCl–ethidium gradients. For two decades this was the way to purify and quantify supercoiled plasmids — and intercalating dyes became the calibrated "wrench" for removing supercoils turn by turn.

Radloff, Bauer & Vinograd, A dye-buoyant-density method for the detection and isolation of closed circular duplex DNA, PNAS 57:1724 (1967) · DOI 10.1073/pnas.57.6.1724

1971 enzyme

Wang purifies ω protein — the first DNA topoisomerase

James C. Wang purifies an E. coli protein, dubbed ω, that removes negative superhelical turns from cccDNA without breaking both strands irreversibly. The reaction proceeds gradually, requires no cofactor, and introduces a reversible "swivel" into DNA. This is the discovery of topoisomerase I, before the name existed.

Wang, Interaction between DNA and Escherichia coli protein ω, J. Mol. Biol. 55:523 (1971) · PubMed search — subsequent purification/characterization: PubMed 817788

1972 enzyme

Mammalian cells have an untwisting enzyme too

Champoux & Dulbecco find a mammalian cell activity that relaxes superhelical DNA — the eukaryotic type I topoisomerase — proposed as the long-sought "swivel" for replication and transcription.

Champoux & Dulbecco, An activity from mammalian cells that untwists superhelical DNA: a possible swivel for DNA replication, PNAS 69:143 (1972) · PubMed 4333036 · DOI 10.1073/pnas.69.1.143 · PMC427563

1975 gel electrophoresis

Nicking–closing enzymes produce a Boltzmann ladder of topoisomers

Depew & Wang treat cccDNA with a nicking–closing enzyme and, crucially, recognize thermally driven conformational fluctuations in the DNA helix: each nicking–closing cycle samples a slightly different winding, so partial relaxation generates a whole distribution of topological isomers — a ladder in which adjacent bands differ by ΔLk = 1, with populations following Boltzmann statistics. This paper is the conceptual bridge between enzyme mechanism and the gel ladders everyone uses today.

Depew & Wang, Conformational fluctuations of DNA helix, PNAS 72:4275 (1975) · PMC388704 · DOI 10.1073/pnas.72.11.4275

1975 gel electrophoresis

Agarose gels count superhelical turns

Keller shows that SV40 DNA topoisomers, previously resolved only by ultracentrifugation, separate cleanly in agarose gel electrophoresis: fully supercoiled DNA runs fastest, and each discrete band corresponds to a species with a defined number of superhelical turns. Gel electrophoresis becomes the standard tool to "read" superhelicity — the direct ancestor of today's plasmid-topology gels.

Keller, Determination of the number of superhelical turns in simian virus 40 DNA by gel electrophoresis, PNAS 72:4876 (1975) · DOI 10.1073/pnas.72.12.4876

1976 enzyme

DNA gyrase: the enzyme that adds supercoils

Gellert, Mizuuchi, O'Dea & Nash discover DNA gyrase — the first enzyme known to introduce negative superhelical turns into DNA in an ATP-dependent reaction. Nature's negative supercoiling (σ ≈ −0.05) is thereby explained as an enzyme activity, not an accident of packaging.

Gellert, Mizuuchi, O'Dea & Nash, DNA gyrase: an enzyme that introduces superhelical turns into DNA, PNAS 73:3872 (1976) · DOI 10.1073/pnas.73.11.3872

1979 gel electrophoresis

Two-dimensional gels and the helical repeat of DNA

Wang's two-dimensional (forward/reverse) gel technique resolves the full topoisomer distribution: DNA is run in one direction, then with an intercalator (chloroquine) added, which unwinds the helix and shifts each topoisomer differently. From the point where the topoisomer distribution reverses, the helical repeat of DNA in solution (~10.5 bp/turn) is measured directly — and each spot on the 2-D arc corresponds to exactly one ΔLk step. This is the experiment that turned topoisomer gels into quantitative topology.

Wang, Helical repeat of DNA in solution, PNAS 76:200 (1979) · PMC382905 · DOI 10.1073/pnas.76.1.200

1979 enzyme

The sign-inversion mechanism of strand passage

Brown & Cozzarelli propose that gyrase supercoils DNA by passing one duplex segment through a transient double-strand break in another — inverting the sign of a crossover — the defining reaction of type II topoisomerases (ΔLk in steps of 2).

Brown & Cozzarelli, A sign inversion mechanism for enzymatic supercoiling of DNA, Science 206:1081 (1979) · DOI 10.1126/science.227059

1979–80 enzyme

A second bacterial type II enzyme, and eukaryotic topo II via catenanes

Brown, Peebles & Cozzarelli purify from E. coli an ATP-dependent topoisomerase related to but distinct from gyrase (now known as topoisomerase IV, the decatenating enzyme of chromosome segregation). In parallel, Baldi et al. isolate from Xenopus eggs a novel ATP-dependent eukaryotic topoisomerase — the discovery route for both was the ability to form and resolve catenanes (interlocked DNA rings), which these enzymes handle via the same double-strand passage reaction.

Brown, Peebles & Cozzarelli, A topoisomerase from Escherichia coli related to DNA gyrase, PNAS 76:6110 (1979) · DOI 10.1073/pnas.76.12.6110
Baldi et al., In vitro catenation and decatenation of DNA and a novel eucaryotic ATP-dependent topoisomerase, Cell 20:85 (1980) · DOI 10.1016/0092-8674(80)90632-7

1981 enzyme

Type I enzymes can catenate and knot too

Brown & Cozzarelli show that even the single-strand-nicking ω protein can pass DNA segments through a transient enzyme-bridged break — revealing a mechanistic unity between type I and type II topoisomerases and making knots and catenanes readouts of enzyme mechanism.

Brown & Cozzarelli, Catenation and knotting of duplex DNA by type 1 topoisomerases: a mechanistic parallel with type 2 topoisomerases, PNAS 78:843 (1981) · DOI 10.1073/pnas.78.2.843

1987 concept

The twin-domain model: transcription generates supercoils

Liu & Wang show that a moving RNA polymerase drags DNA around it, creating a twin-domain of positive supercoils ahead and negative supercoils behind — explaining why cells need topoisomerases everywhere, not just at replication forks.

Liu & Wang, Supercoiling of DNA during transcription, PNAS 84:7024 (1987) · DOI 10.1073/pnas.84.20.7024

1996 synthesis

The field's canonical review

Wang's Annual Reviews article consolidates the discovery arc — ω, gyrase, catenanes, sign inversion — into the modern classification of type I/II topoisomerases. An excellent single entry point to the whole story.

Wang, DNA topoisomerases, Annu. Rev. Biochem. 65:635 (1996) · PubMed 8811192 · DOI 10.1146/annurev.bi.65.070196.003223

3 · Interactive lab: read a topoisomer gel

1-D agarose gel

2-D (forward / reverse) topoisomer gel

After the first dimension, chloroquine is added for the second run. Each spot is one topoisomer (ΔLk = 1 apart). Because the intercalator unwinds the helix, the distribution bends into an arc — the minimum of the arc marks the topoisomer that is relaxed under those dye conditions, and the arc's shape yields the solution helical repeat (~10.5 bp/turn), exactly as in Wang's 1979 forward/reverse gels.

4 · Glossary

  • Linking number (Lk) — integer count of times one strand crosses the other; fixed in covalently closed DNA.
  • Twist (Tw) / Writhe (Wr) — the geometric split of Lk into helical winding and supercoiling.
  • Topoisomers — same DNA molecule, different Lk; differ by ΔLk = 1 (type I enzymes) or 2 (type II).
  • Superhelix density (σ) — normalized supercoiling: σ = ΔLk/Lk₀; natural plasmids ≈ −0.05.
  • Topoisomerase I (ω) — single-strand passage, ΔLk = ±1, ATP-independent (Wang 1971).
  • DNA gyrase — bacterial type II enzyme that actively introduces negative supercoils, ΔLk = −2 (Gellert 1976).
  • Topoisomerase IV — bacterial type II enzyme specialized for decatenation (Brown 1979).
  • Catenanes & knots — interlocked and knotted circles; natural readouts of strand passage.
  • Ethidium bromide / chloroquine — intercalators that unwind DNA, shifting every topoisomer's mobility; the basis of dye titration and 2-D gels.
  • Boltzmann distribution — the near-Gaussian spread of topoisomer amounts produced by partial relaxation (Depew & Wang 1975).
