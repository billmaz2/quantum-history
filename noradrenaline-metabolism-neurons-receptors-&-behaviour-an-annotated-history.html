1 · A brief history

  • 1895–1901 — Oliver & Schäfer discover the pressor action of adrenal gland extract; Takamine and Aldrich isolate and crystallize its active principle, named adrenaline. Elliott (1904) proposes that sympathetic nerves act by releasing an "adrenaline-like" substance — the chemical-transmission hypothesis is born. [1]
  • 1921 — Otto Loewi demonstrates chemical transmission of the heart by "Vagusstoff" (acetylcholine) and "Acceleranstoff", opening the era in which sympathetic transmitters could be sought. (Loewi & Dale share the 1936 Nobel Prize.)
  • 1946 — Ulf von Euler identifies noradrenaline as the sympathetic neurotransmitter — not adrenaline — in adrenergic nerve extracts, work recognized with the 1970 Nobel Prize.
  • 1948 — Ahlquist proposes two adrenergic receptor populations, α and β, from rank orders of agonist potency — the founding classification of adrenergic pharmacology. [3]
  • 1954 — Marthe Vogt maps noradrenaline in the brain by bioassay and shows its uneven, behaviour-relevant distribution — the start of central noradrenaline neurochemistry. [4]
  • 1962–65 — Falck & Hillarp develop the formaldehyde-induced histofluorescence method; Dahlström & Fuxe (1964) map the central catecholamine neurons into cell groups A1–A15, the noradrenergic groups being A1–A7 with the locus coeruleus (A6) as the great forebrain source. [5]
  • 1962–67 — Black and colleagues synthesize propranolol, the first clinically useful β-blocker — the translation of Ahlquist's classification into cardiovascular medicine (Nobel Prize, 1988); Lands subdivides β receptors into β1/β2 by agonist rank order. [6], [7]
  • 1974–77 — Langer, Starke and Berthelsen & Pettinger establish α2 autoreceptors on noradrenergic terminals and the α1/α2 subdivision; clonidine, the α2 agonist, enters medicine as an antihypertensive. [8], [9]
  • 1970s — Tricyclic antidepressants are shown to inhibit noradrenaline uptake (desipramine as the selective noradrenaline reuptake inhibitor); the catecholamine hypothesis of affective disorders (Schildkraut, 1965) ties noradrenaline to mood. [10]
  • 1981 — Aston-Jones & Bloom describe the two firing modes of locus coeruleus neurons — tonic slow activity vs phasic bursts — coupling the noradrenergic system to vigilance, attention and behavioural flexibility. [11]
  • 1986–91 — The molecular era: Dixon, Kobilka, Lefkowitz and colleagues clone the β2 (1986), α2 (1987), β1 (1987), α1 (1988) and β3 (1989) receptors; Amara and Pacholczyk clone the noradrenaline transporter NET (1991) — all G-protein-coupled, with β-arrestin desensitization by Lefkowitz's group (Nobel Prize, 2012). [12], [13], [14]
  • 1987–90s — The antidepressant pipeline: SNRIs (venlafaxine, 1993; duloxetine) join the tricyclics and mirtazapine's α2-blockade in modulating noradrenergic transmission; β-blockers (propranolol) are adopted for performance anxiety and PTSD reconsolidation blockade. [15]
  • 1998–2000s — NET knockout mice (Xu, Gainetdinov, Caron) show tachycardia, aggression and altered antidepressant response; DBH-null mice and human DBH deficiency define the noradrenaline-only phenotype; and α2 agonists are established in ADHD (guanfacine). [16], [17], [18]

2 · Metabolism and the enzymes of the pathway

Noradrenaline is synthesised from L-tyrosine through the catecholamine line: dopamine is made first, then converted to noradrenaline inside the vesicle by dopamine β-hydroxylase. It is stored by VMAT2, inactivated by oxidative deamination and O-methylation, and cleared from the synapse chiefly by the plasma-membrane transporter NET:

Enzymes

Tyrosine hydroxylase & AADC (shared with dopamine)

The first two catecholamine steps are shared with the dopamine pathway: TH, the BH4-dependent rate-limiting hydroxylation to L-DOPA, and AADC, the pyridoxal-dependent decarboxylation to dopamine. Their pharmacology (α-methyl-p-tyrosine, carbidopa) is therefore common to dopamine and noradrenaline neurons.

Dopamine β-hydroxylase (DBH)

The ascorbate- and copper-dependent vesicular enzyme that converts dopamine to noradrenaline inside the storage granule — the defining step of the noradrenergic neuron. DBH is released with noradrenaline upon exocytosis; CSF/plasma DBH is a classical marker of noradrenergic output. Recessive DBH deficiency produces severe orthostatic hypotension and ptosis with normal dopamine function — a pure noradrenaline knockout phenotype, fully responsive to L-DOPS (droxidopa). [17]

Monoamine oxidase A + COMT

Noradrenaline is inactivated mainly by MAO-A: oxidative deamination to the aldehyde, then aldehyde reductase/dehydrogenase to MHPG in brain (the CSF and urinary turnover index) or, via the adrenal route, to VMA. COMT O-methylates NA to normetanephrine. The MAOI antidepressants act here — the first modern antidepressants after iproniazid, tying noradrenaline (with serotonin) to mood. [10]

Phenylethanolamine N-methyltransferase (PNMT)

The cortisol-induced enzyme that methylates noradrenaline to adrenaline — found essentially only in the adrenal medulla (chromaffin cells) and a few brainstem (C1/C2/C3) populations. In the periphery noradrenaline is thus a transmitter and adrenaline a hormone; PNMT expression is induced by glucocorticoids from the adjacent cortex.

Note: peripheral noradrenaline is a sympathetic transmitter (released at every effector — heart, vasculature, gut, sweat glands), and plasma NA is spillover, not synaptic leak; brain NA is a separate pool behind the blood–brain barrier synthesized in situ. Unlike dopamine (mainly nigrostriatal) or serotonin (mainly peripheral), the brain's noradrenaline comes overwhelmingly from a single compact structure: the locus coeruleus.

3 · Anatomy: the noradrenergic cell groups A1–A7

The central noradrenergic system arises from ~10,000–15,000 neurons per human brainstem, mapped by Dahlström & Fuxe (1964) into groups A1–A7 [5]. The locus coeruleus alone (A6) supplies most forebrain noradrenaline; the definitive modern account is Foote, Bloom & Aston-Jones' Annual Review of Neuroscience monograph and Aston-Jones's work on LC function [11].

Physiology: LC neurons fire in two modes (Aston-Jones & Bloom, 1981) — slow tonic activity (1–2 Hz) supporting vigilance, and phasic bursts locked to salient/decision-relevant events with post-burst pauses; stress and novelty shift the system toward high tonic activity (distractibility, scanning), while low tonic activity accompanies drowsiness and off-task states [11]. LC firing is inhibited by α2A somatodendritic autoreceptors (clonidine/medetomidine) — the mechanism exploited in antihypertensives, sedation and ADHD treatment — and by opiate α2-mimetic action in the LC, the key locus of opiate withdrawal.

4 · Receptor subtypes and behavioural correlates

All adrenergic receptors are G-protein-coupled, descending from Ahlquist's α/β division (1948) [3] through the α1/α2 (1974–77) and β1/β2 (1967) subdivisions into nine cloned human subtypes: three α1 (α1A/B/D, Gq), three α2 (α2A/B/C, Gi/o) and three β (β1/2/3, Gs) [12], [16].

Transporters complete the picture: NET (SLC6A2) clears synaptic noradrenaline — the site of action of tricyclics (desipramine), the selective reuptake inhibitor reboxetine, and the SNRIs venlafaxine and duloxetine, and a target of cocaine and methylphenidate. NET-null mice show the predicted tachycardia, cold intolerance and altered antidepressant response [16]. NET also clears dopamine in the prefrontal cortex, where dopamine transporters are scarce — a fact exploited by methylphenidate in ADHD.

5 · Lesion and depletion studies

Pharmacological depletion

  • Reserpine — the VMAT2 blocker that empties noradrenaline (with dopamine and serotonin) from storage vesicles; reserpine-induced sedation and "psychic depression" in patients was one of the founding observations of the catecholamine hypothesis of depression. [10]
  • α-Methyl-p-tyrosine (AMPT) — TH inhibition depletes catecholamine synthesis; in humans, AMPT transiently induces depressive symptoms in remitted patients and blunts reward, tying noradrenaline synthesis to mood maintenance. [10]
  • Fusaric acid / DBH inhibitors — dopamine β-hydroxylase inhibition selectively removes noradrenaline while dopamine accumulates; the experimental complement to human DBH deficiency. [17]
  • Yohimbine / clonidine challenge — α2 blockade or α2 agonism tests the sensitivity of the α2 autoreceptor in panic and mood research; yohimbine-induced anxiety is the standard human noradrenergic-stress provocation.

Neurotoxic and genetic lesions

  • DSP-4 — the systemically administered neurotoxin taken up by NET that selectively destroys locus coeruleus noradrenergic terminals; the standard selective-lesion tool for mapping central NA function ( spared by NET blockade). [10]
  • 6-Hydroxydopamine (6-OHDA) — with desipramine pretreatment to spare noradrenergic neurons, it becomes the dopamine-selective lesion; without protection, LC/terminal 6-OHDA lesions deplete noradrenaline — the classic pharmacological dissection of the two catecholamine systems.
  • Electrolytic/excitotoxic LC lesions — forebrain NA depletion blunts arousal, cortical responsiveness to novel stimuli, stress-induced anxiety and the α2-agonist withdrawal syndrome; LC loss in Alzheimer's disease (Braak's staging) correlates with the early attentional decline.
  • Genetic "lesions" of the phenotype — DBH-null mice and human DBH deficiency (Robertson's kindred) lack noradrenaline and adrenaline entirely but have normal dopamine — proving NA is required for cardiovascular homeostasis, not for survival [17]; NET knockout mice (Xu et al. 2000) are tachycardic, cold-intolerant, aggressive and show altered antidepressant response [16]; Phox2a/Phox2b transcription factors are required for the development of all noradrenergic (autonomic) neurons.
  • Human noradrenaline pharmacology as "lesion" — β-blockade (propranolol) disrupts the peripheral arousal component of emotional memory (reconsolidation studies in PTSD and fear conditioning) [18]; MHPG in CSF/urine remains the classical output measure of central NA turnover in depression research.
