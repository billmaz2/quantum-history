How the Citric Acid Cycle Was Discovered

A conceptual and experimental reconstruction, 1910–1953 — from the great oxidation debate to Hans Krebs's pigeon-muscle manometry, the acetyl-CoA revolution, and the Nobel Prize.

Each section presents the dominant concept of its time, the experimental anomaly that challenged it, the new concept that emerged, and the actual experiments — with the logic Krebs himself used: if a substance is a normal intermediate, adding it should catalytically increase the respiration rate of living tissue.

The central question of 1910: how does food burn?

By 1900 it was known that cells "combust" food to CO₂ and water, releasing energy. But how? Two rival theories fought for decades: Warburg's "activation of oxygen" (the cell activates O₂ itself) and Wieland's "activation of hydrogen" (oxidation is really dehydrogenation — removing hydrogen atoms stepwise). The citric acid cycle was assembled, piece by piece, from experiments testing these theories — and from a stubborn problem: the intermediates of carbohydrate oxidation simply could not be found.

11910s · Lund — the hydrogen-transport toolkit

Thunberg's Dehydrogenases and the Methylene-Blue Test

Old idea
Biological oxidation was thought to be a direct reaction between foodstuff and molecular oxygen, catalyzed by "oxidases" that add oxygen atoms to substrates.
The problem
Oxygen is a sluggish oxidant at body temperature; foodstuffs are stable in air. Something must activate one or the other partner — but what, and how could it be studied without oxygen interfering?
The experiment
Thorsten Thunberg developed the methylene blue test: a sealed evacuated tube in which tissue suspension + substrate + methylene blue are incubated. Methylene blue is a hydrogen acceptor — when it takes up hydrogen, its blue color fades to colorless. Color loss therefore directly visualizes hydrogen transfer, with no oxygen involved. Using this tool Thunberg discovered a family of dehydrogenases — enzymes that remove hydrogen from specific substrates — and introduced the terms "hydrogen donator" and "hydrogen acceptor" that are still used today. Succinic acid proved a spectacular hydrogen donator in muscle.
New concept
Wieland's dehydrogenation theory gained an experimental engine: oxidation = stepwise hydrogen removal by specific dehydrogenases, with the hydrogen passed to acceptors. Warburg's and Wieland's camps would later be reconciled in the respiratory chain — hydrogen (as electrons) travels through carriers to oxygen. Thunberg's test would become one of the key tools that identified the very intermediates of the future citric acid cycle.

21910s–30s · Berlin & Cambridge — the manometric era begins

Warburg's Tissue Respiration and the Barcroft–Warburg Manometer

Old idea
Respiration could only be studied in intact animals or organs — too complex to isolate individual reactions.
The problem
To find the intermediates of oxidation, one needs a quantitative measure of respiration in a controllable tissue preparation — measuring O₂ consumption and CO₂ output minute by minute with added candidate substrates.
The experiments & tools
Otto Warburg developed tissue-slice respiration measurements and the "Warburg manometer" (with Barcroft's independent design), which measures gas pressure changes in a closed vessel — turning respiration into a precise physical measurement. Warburg's heavy-metal catalysis theory and his iron-containing "Atmungsferment" (cytochrome oxidase) laid the basis of the respiratory chain. Krebs learned manometry in Warburg's Berlin laboratory (1930–31) as a Rockefeller fellow — this single technique became the backbone of every experiment in the cycle's discovery.
New concept
Respiration became a measurable, manipulable property of tissue suspensions. The central experimental logic of the whole field was set: slice/dice the tissue, incubate it in buffer, add a candidate intermediate, and measure whether O₂ consumption rises. A true intermediate increases respiration far beyond its own oxidation — catalytically.
31935–36 · Szeged — the C4-dicarboxylic acid catalysts

Szent-Györgyi's Fumarate Catalysis — Four Acids That Carry Hydrogen

Old idea
The intermediates of carbohydrate oxidation were assumed to be unknown, still-unidentified carbon compounds; the known small acids of the cell (succinate, fumarate, malate) were viewed as side-products of little importance.
The problem
In Szeged, Szent-Györgyi's group found that minced-muscle respiration depended on dialyzable, heat-stable factors. When they tested known cell acids, something remarkable appeared: tiny amounts of fumaric, malic, and succinic acids massively accelerated respiration — acting like catalysts, not fuels. In their experiments, only a few micromoles of C4-dicarboxylic acids enabled the oxidation of many hundred times more substrate.
The experiments
Using the manometric method, they showed oxaloacetate → malate → fumarate → succinate interconversions in muscle, each handing hydrogen to the next: the cell's carbohydrate donates hydrogen to oxaloacetate (making malate), which reduces fumarate to succinate, which then passes hydrogen on toward the respiratory chain (via the newly discovered "cytoflav" — flavin nucleotides, the yellow enzyme system). The four acids were being regenerated, not consumed.
New concept
Here was a first partial cycle: hydrogen transport catalysis by C4-dicarboxylic acids. Szent-Györgyi received the 1937 Nobel Prize "for his discoveries in connection with the biological combustion processes, with special reference to vitamin C and the catalysis of fumaric acid." But his scheme explained only hydrogen transfer — it could not account for where the carbon went, or where the CO₂ came from. That was Krebs's problem.
oxaloacetate →(2H) malate →(−H₂O) fumarate →(2H) succinate →(2H) → respiratory chainSzent-Györgyi's C4 hydrogen-transport chain (1935–36). Note it is an open chain, not yet a cycle — and note which reaction is missing: succinate → oxaloacetate. That gap would eventually be filled by the cycle's discovery.

41937 · Tübingen — citrate enters the picture

Martius & Knoop — The Tricarboxylic Acid Route: Citrate → Isocitrate → α-Ketoglutarate

Old idea
Citric acid — famously abundant in citrus fruit — was considered a plant product with no significant role in animal respiration.
The problem
Where does citrate come from in animal tissues, and can it be oxidized? Franz Knoop (already famous for the β-oxidation theory of fatty acids from 1904) suspected that the "wasted" citric acid of the body might be oxidized through a specific chain of tricarboxylic acids.
The experiments
In 1937 Carl Martius and Franz Knoop, working with tissue enzymes, demonstrated the reaction sequence citrate → cis-aconitate → isocitrate → α-ketoglutarate, discovering the enzyme aconitase (aconitate hydratase) and the oxidative decarboxylation of isocitrate to α-ketoglutarate (with CO₂ release — the source of some of the respired carbon!). Their work supplied the complete upper half of the future cycle — an open chain, not yet connected to the C4 acids.
New concept
For the first time, a tricarboxylic acid route existed: citrate was not a dead-end plant product but a metabolized intermediate of animal respiration, and the chain of Martius & Knoop converted a C6 tricarboxylic acid into a C5 compound (α-ketoglutarate), releasing CO₂. What nobody yet knew was how this chain connected to Szent-Györgyi's C4 acids.
citrate →(aconitase) cis-aconitate →(aconitase) isocitrate →(isocitrate dehydrogenase + NAD⁺) α-ketoglutarate + CO₂ + NADHThe Martius–Knoop route (1937). First CO₂ now has a mechanistic source. First CO₂ now has a mechanistic source.
51937 · Sheffield — the synthesis of the cycle

Krebs & Johnson — The Manometric Proof of a Cycle

Old idea
Respiration was thought to proceed through linear chains of intermediates (Szent-Györgyi's hydrogen chain; Martius–Knoop's tricarboxylic chain). No one had proposed that the intermediates of a major metabolic route form a closed loop.
The problem
Hans Krebs — a Jewish physician expelled from Freiburg in 1933, who had rebuilt his laboratory in Sheffield on pigeon breast muscle (chosen because its extraordinary flight metabolism makes respiration measurements clean and vigorous) — faced a puzzle: which substances increase respiration of minced muscle, and which are consumed vs. regenerated? The key clues: (1) all the C4 and C6 acids stimulated respiration catalytically; (2) citrate disappeared in muscle only if the C4 acids were also present; (3) succinate was oxidized rapidly — but its oxidation was specifically and competitively inhibited by malonate, an analogue of succinate. This malonate experiment was the masterstroke: if succinate dehydrogenase is blocked by malonate, then succinate accumulates when any of the other acids (citrate, isocitrate, α-ketoglutarate, fumarate, malate, oxaloacetate) is metabolized — proving they all feed into succinate. The chain had to be a loop.
The experiments (Krebs & Johnson 1937)
With manometric measurements of O₂ uptake and CO₂ output in minced pigeon breast muscle: (a) adding 3 µmol citrate caused extra O₂ consumption of ~80 µmol — a 30-fold catalytic amplification, impossible if citrate were merely a fuel; (b) the ratio of CO₂ released to O₂ consumed matched a cycle of one CO₂ per turn per C2 unit oxidized; (c) in the presence of malonate, all the other cycle intermediates accumulated as succinate; (d) oxaloacetate + an unknown "active C2 fragment" (from carbohydrate) was inferred to condense to citrate — the missing entry reaction. Krebs and Johnson assembled the full sequence of seven intermediates, and Krebs proposed it as a closed cycle in which oxaloacetate is regenerated after each turn — a totally new concept for metabolism.
New concept
The citric acid cycle: carbohydrate-derived C2 units are oxidized to CO₂ inside a circular sequence in which the C4 acids act catalytically, are regenerated, and carry the hydrogens to the respiratory chain. The 1937 paper "The role of citric acid in intermediate metabolism in animal tissues" (Krebs & Johnson, Enzymologia) is one of the most cited papers in the history of biochemistry. Krebs tested the scheme for quantitative consistency — the rates, the CO₂ ratios, the malonate block — and, characteristically, performed a final "paper chemistry" test: he enumerated all known cell acids and checked whether the new scheme uniquely explained the catalytic effects. It did.
Cycle (1937 form):
oxaloacetate + [C2 unit] → citrate → cis-aconitate → isocitrate → α-ketoglutarate → succinate → fumarate → malate → oxaloacetate
With each turn: one C2 unit fully oxidized to 2 CO₂, hydrogens delivered to the respiratory chain, and the C4 "carrier" regenerated — catalysis by a circle. (Today's textbook form: oxaloacetate + acetyl-CoA → citrate → … → oxaloacetate.)

61941–53 · New York, Boston & Munich — the missing C2 donor found

Lipmann, Lynen, Ochoa — "Active Acetate" Becomes Acetyl-CoA

Old idea
In 1937, the C2 unit entering the cycle was a black box — Krebs called it an "active acetate" or "active C2 fragment," knowing only that it came from carbohydrate (and, surprisingly, also from fat and protein breakdown).
The problem
Every attempt to identify "active acetate" with a known molecule (acetate itself, acetophosphate, acetyl phosphate) failed. The Condensing reaction — oxaloacetate + C2 → citrate — could not be studied until the C2 carrier was known. Meanwhile Krebs had noticed (1937–40s) that carbohydrates, fats and proteins all entered this same cycle: one common terminal pathway of oxidation — but how?
The experiments
Fritz Lipmann discovered coenzyme A (CoA) (1945; the "A" for acetylation) while studying acetylation in pigeon liver extracts; and in 1951 Feodor Lynen in Munich isolated acetyl-CoA from yeast — the mysterious "active acetate" was acetate bound by a thioester (–S–) linkage to CoA, a high-energy bond. Severo Ochoa's group (Stern, Shapiro, Stadtman; with Lynen, 1951–52) then isolated the "condensing enzyme" (citrate synthase) from pig heart and demonstrated the exact entry reaction in a test tube: acetyl-CoA + oxaloacetate → citrate + CoA. The last black box of the 1937 scheme was opened — and the way fat and amino acids fed into the cycle (through acetyl-CoA) became mechanistically clear.
New concept
The unified picture: all three foodstuffs converge on acetyl-CoA, and the cycle oxidizes it. Lipmann shared the 1953 Nobel Prize with Krebs "for his discovery of co-enzyme A and its importance for intermediary metabolism"; Lynen received his own Nobel Prize in 1964.
acetyl-CoA + oxaloacetate + H₂O →(citrate synthase) citrate + CoA-SHThe condensing reaction, finally demonstrated enzymatically in 1951–52 (Stern, Ochoa, Lynen). "Citric acid is not just a metabolite — it is the product of a biosynthetic condensation."

71948–57 · Chicago & Baltimore — the cycle finds its home

Kennedy & Lehninger — The Cycle Moves Into the Mitochondrion

Old idea
The cycle enzymes were assumed to float free in the soluble cytoplasm; the cell's oxidation machinery was pictured as a homogeneous soup of enzymes.
The problem
Krebs's minced-muscle preparations mixed everything together. But if the cycle ran in a cell organelle, its kinetics, its coupling to the respiratory chain, and its regulation could be fundamentally different — and experiments in homogenates were hard to interpret.
The experiments
Using the newly developed technique of cell fractionation by differential centrifugation (Claude, Hogeboom, Schneider — 1946–48), Albert Lehninger and Eugene Kennedy (1948–49) showed that fatty-acid oxidation and the entire citric acid cycle — together with the respiratory chain and oxidative phosphorylation — are located in the mitochondria, and that the cycle's oxidation only proceeds with the coupled phosphorylation of ADP. Later work by Green, Lehninger, Slater, Chance and others mapped the carriers (NADH, flavins, cytochromes, iron-sulfur proteins) into the respiratory chain that consumes the hydrogens which the cycle's dehydrogenases strip off.
New concept
The mitochondrion is the cell's oxidative power plant: the citric acid cycle, the respiratory chain and ATP synthesis are physically and functionally coupled in one organelle. This set the stage for Mitchell's chemiosmotic theory (1961, Nobel 1978) — but that is another story.
81948 · Oxford — the symmetry paradox resolved

Ogston's Three-Point Attachment — How a Symmetric Molecule Behaves Asymmetrically

Old idea
Citric acid is a symmetric molecule (a prochiral carbon with two identical –CH₂COOH arms). From the symmetric citrate, aconitase "should" act on either arm at random — and the products should be symmetric mixtures. Yet experiments kept showing that citrate formed from labeled precursors yielded asymmetric products.
The problem
By 1948, isotope-labeling experiments (e.g., with ¹⁴CO₂ and labeled acetate) produced an embarrassing paradox: citrate behaved as if its two "identical" arms were distinguishable. Either citrate was not a true intermediate (and some proposed replacing it!), or enzymes could somehow tell the arms apart.
The experiment & argument
Alexander Ogston's 1948 paper in Nature argued — in just one page — that when a symmetric molecule binds to an asymmetric enzyme surface at three points, its two identical arms become topologically distinct: the enzyme always orients the substrate the same way, so products are asymmetric. Experimental confirmation (Potter & Heidelberger and others, 1949–50) showed that enzymes indeed distinguish the arms of citrate. The cycle's citrate problem was solved — the paradox disappeared, and the citric acid cycle survived its greatest conceptual crisis.
New concept
Prochiral discrimination by enzymes — one of the most beautiful insights of enzymology, and a foundational lesson of what became bioorganic chemistry: enzymes see molecules in three dimensions; chemical symmetry on paper does not equal biochemical symmetry.
Key papers
  • Ogston AG. Interpretation of experiments on metabolic processes, with special reference to the citric acid cycle. Nature 162, 4129 (1948).

91949–53 · Oxford — the poison that proved the pathway

Fluorocitrate and "Lethal Synthesis" — Poison as Proof

Old idea
Metabolic poisons were mostly tools of accidental poisoning; their mechanisms were unknown.
The problem
Sodium fluoroacetate, used as a rodenticide in Britain after WWII, killed animals with a delay — and poisoned tissues accumulated enormous amounts of citrate. Why would a poison cause citrate accumulation, unless citrate stood at the center of a pathway that the poison was blocking?
The experiments
Sir Rudolph Peters and colleagues (1949–1953) established the concept of "lethal synthesis": the cell itself converts fluoroacetate (harmless) via the cycle's own machinery into fluorocitrate (deadly), because acetyl-CoA synthetase and citrate synthase cannot distinguish fluoroacetate from acetate. Fluorocitrate then specifically inhibits aconitase, the enzyme Martius & Knoop discovered — blocking citrate → isocitrate and causing citrate to pile up. The block exactly matched the predicted point in the cycle, and Peters' group synthesized the fluorotricarboxylic acid to prove its identity.
New concept
A poison converted by the pathway's own enzymes into a specific inhibitor became a powerful confirmation of the cycle's sequence and of the biosynthetic entry reaction. "Lethal synthesis" (Peters' term) became a classic pharmacological concept, and fluorocitrate became a standard laboratory tool for isolating citrate synthase's product and studying aconitase.
Key papers

101953 · Stockholm — recognition

The Nobel Prize — and the Cycle's Legacy

In 1953 the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was shared by Hans Adolf Krebs "for his discovery of the citric acid cycle" and Fritz Lipmann "for his discovery of co-enzyme A and its importance for intermediary metabolism." Krebs's Nobel lecture traces the full intellectual history summarized on this page — and characteristically spends as much time on what was not known as on the discovery itself.

The cycle quickly became the "hub of the wheel" of metabolism — the common terminal pathway not just of carbohydrates, but of fats and amino acids; the source of NADH powering oxidative phosphorylation; and, later (with its reversed roles in gluconeogenesis and its reductive version in bacteria), one of the most ancient metabolic constructions in biology. The discovery is also a historical landmark in methodology: it demonstrated that a complete metabolic pathway could be reconstructed from careful manometric experiments, inhibitors, catalytic logic, and sheer integrative reasoning — before a single pathway enzyme was purified, and a decade before anything was known of its cellular location or regulation.
