The Krebs Cycle & the Glycogen Cycle as Systems

How systems biology turned two classic biochemical diagrams into dynamic, predictive control systems. The Krebs (TCA) cycle and glycogen metabolism were mapped as reaction schemes by classical biochemistry (1930s–1950s). Systems biology asks different questions: which step controls the flux? why is the network robust? when does it oscillate or switch?

Each section below follows the same structure: the classical (static) view, the question it could not answer, the systems-level model and finding, and the key equations — with historical papers and working free-access references.

The paradigm shift: from reaction maps to control systems

Classical biochemistry gave us the "what": which enzymes, which metabolites, which arrows. Systems biology asks about the "how much" and "when": metabolic fluxes (rates through pathways), control coefficients (how much each enzyme controls the flux), dynamics (oscillations, switches, transients), and robustness (why the network keeps working despite perturbations). The two pathways in this page illustrate both major branches of the field: the cybernetic / control-theory tradition (glycogen regulation as an ultrasensitive switch — Goldbeter, Koshland) and the kinetic modeling / flux analysis tradition (TCA cycle as an ODE system and flux network — from early kinetic models to ¹³C temporal fluxomics and genome-scale methods).

11930s–1950s · The classical foundations

Mapping the Cycles — Krebs, Cori & Cori, Sutherland

The classical view
Hans Krebs mapped the citric acid cycle (1937) from pigeon-flight muscle experiments — five reactions that oxidize carbon while regenerating their own carrier. Carl and Gerty Cori mapped the glycogen ↔ glucose corridor and the "Cori cycle" between liver and muscle. The diagrams were static: enzymes as labeled arrows, regulation as on/off notes.
What classical biochemistry could not answer
Textbooks spoke of a single "rate-limiting step" per pathway — but which one? Do all steps just follow their substrate concentrations, or does regulation create switches and amplifiers? Why does adding more of a "bottleneck" enzyme often barely change the flux? Static maps cannot answer any dynamical question: they say nothing about response time, stability, amplification, or the effect of changing multiple enzymes at once.
The systems view begins
Already in the 1940s–60s, the discovery of allosteric regulation (Monod, Changeux, Jacob 1963), reversible phosphorylation of glycogen phosphorylase (Krebs & Fischer, 1955–56 — the first protein phosphorylation cascade ever discovered), and the cAMP second-messenger cascade (Sutherland, Nobel 1971) revealed that metabolic pathways are wired into multi-layer control circuits — precisely the kind of object that control theory and later systems biology were built to analyze.
glycogen phosphorylase: phosphorylase-b ⇌ phosphorylase-a  ·  cAMP → PKA → phosphorylase kinase → phosphorylaseThe glycogen cascade discovered by Krebs & Fischer (1956, Nobel 1992) and Sutherland: a hormone signal amplified through covalent modification. One hormone molecule activates many PKA molecules, each activating many kinase molecules, each activating many phosphorylase molecules — a biological amplifier chain.
21973–74 · The founding theory: metabolic control analysis

Kacser & Burns, Heinrich & Rapoport — Nobody is "the rate-limiting enzyme"

Old idea
The "rate-limiting step" doctrine: each pathway has one bottleneck enzyme that controls the flux; increasing that enzyme increases flux proportionally.
The problem
Experiments kept contradicting the doctrine. Overexpressing a putative "rate-limiting" enzyme often changed flux barely at all; the regulatory properties of a single enzyme told you nothing about the pathway's behavior. A theory was needed for how control is distributed across a network.
New model
Metabolic Control Analysis (MCA), created independently by Kacser & Burns (1973) and Heinrich & Rapoport (1974): control is a shared, quantifiable property of the whole system. Each enzyme gets a dimensionless flux control coefficient CJ,i — the fractional change in pathway flux caused by a fractional change in that enzyme's activity. The famous summation theorem says all coefficients in a pathway sum to exactly 1. No single enzyme is "the" controller; control shifts with conditions. MCA became the mathematical backbone of systems biology, and its central result — control redistribution — explains why single-enzyme overexpression experiments usually disappoint.
CJ,i = (∂J/J) / (∂ei/ei)   ·   Σi CJ,i = 1Flux control coefficient and the summation theorem (Kacser & Burns 1973; Heinrich & Rapoport 1974). For the TCA cycle this means flux control is distributed among many steps and shifts with substrate supply, energy charge and hormonal state — a fact now confirmed by ¹³C flux experiments.

31981–87 · Glycogen regulation as an ultrasensitive switch

Goldbeter & Koshland — Zero-Order Ultrasensitivity in the Glycogen Cascade

Old idea
Covalent modification (phosphorylation/dephosphorylation) was thought to give smooth, graded responses: a little more hormone, a little more active phosphorylase.
The problem
But organisms need switches: liver must synthesize glycogen after a meal and break it down while fasting — simultaneously running both is wasted energy (a futile cycle) and dangerous. How can a smooth chemical cascade produce an all-or-none decision? Classical kinetics of a single enzyme cannot answer this.
New model & findings
Goldbeter & Koshland (1981) showed theoretically that when the modifying enzymes (kinases, phosphatases) are saturated with their protein substrate ("zero-order" regime), a covalent modification cycle behaves like an ultrasensitive switch: the response curve becomes effectively sigmoidal, with Hill coefficients far above 1. Meinke, Bishop & Edstrom (1986) then showed experimentally that glycogen phosphorylase activation is exactly such a zero-order ultrasensitive switch — and quantified the whole glycogen cascade system, finding that liver glycogen synthase and muscle phosphorylase achieve ultrasensitivity through distinctive regulatory designs. In 1996, Cárdenas & Goldbeter built the theoretical model of the liver's glucose-induced switch between glycogen phosphorylase and glycogen synthase: glucose itself, by inhibiting phosphorylase-a, flips liver from glycogen breakdown to synthesis. Later work extended the model to Ca²⁺/IP₃-driven phosphorylase dynamics. This line of research is a pillar of the systems-biology tradition: treating a metabolic pathway as a signal-processing circuit with amplification, thresholds and memory.
va = V1W(1 − va) / [V1W(1 − va) + V2va]  →  nH >> 1 (zero-order regime)The Goldbeter–Koshland covalent-modification cycle (1981): when converter enzymes are saturated (W large), the fraction of modified protein va switches almost discontinuously with signal — "zero-order ultrasensitivity." Applied experimentally to glycogen phosphorylase by Meinke et al. (1986).

41990s–2020s · The Krebs cycle as an ODE system

Kinetic Models of the TCA Cycle — ODEs, Databases & Even Queueing Theory

Old idea
The Krebs cycle as a wall chart: eight reactions, memorized individually, with no notion of how the whole thing behaves as a dynamical system.
The problem
Real TCA flux depends on the NAD⁺/NADH ratio, substrate supply, ATP demand, Ca²⁺ activation of dehydrogenases, and product inhibition — all coupled, all simultaneous. Which steps carry the control (MCA question)? What happens during transients — exercise, feeding, hypoxia? Static maps cannot compute anything.
New model
Kinetic models write the cycle (plus entry points and the respiratory chain) as a set of coupled ordinary differential equations with enzyme rate laws (Michaelis–Menten with allosteric modifiers), then solve for dynamics, control coefficients and stability. These models live in community-curated, machine-readable databases — BioModels and WikiPathways — with standard formats (SBML). A 2012 paper explicitly reviewed how to improve the description of the human TCA cycle for computational use. On the wilder side, a 2021 Bioinformatics paper modeled the Krebs cycle with queueing theory (the mathematics of waiting lines) as a stochastic Markov process — avoiding the negative-concentration artifacts of naive ODEs — and the same group later integrated the Krebs cycle, glycolysis, the pentose phosphate pathway and fatty-acid β-oxidation into a single queueing-theory model of central carbon metabolism. Pancreatic β-cell models now routinely embed a kinetic TCA cycle (plus glycolysis, PPP, glutaminolysis, ETC) to explain insulin secretion dynamics.
d[Xi]/dt = Σj νij vj(X, parameters)   ·   v = Vmax[S]/(Km + [S])The core of every kinetic metabolic model: each metabolite's concentration changes according to the balance of reaction rates (stoichiometry ν), and each rate follows enzyme kinetics with allosteric modifiers. For the TCA cycle, modifiers like NADH inhibition of dehydrogenases and ATP/ADP ratios make the system highly nonlinear.

52017 · The living TCA cycle oscillates

Temporal Fluxomics — TCA Flux Oscillates Through the Cell Cycle

Old idea
¹³C metabolic flux analysis (MFA) assumed a steady state: feed cells labeled glucose, wait, measure isotope patterns, infer constant fluxes. The TCA cycle was treated as a steady machine humming at a fixed rate.
The problem
In a proliferating cell there is no true steady state: enzyme levels, ATP demand and substrate supply all change as the cell grows and divides. Steady-state MFA averages over — and therefore hides — any dynamics.
New model & finding
Ahn & Antoniewicz (2017, Molecular Systems Biology) developed temporal (pseudo-steady-state) fluxomics: synchronizing mammalian cells by cell cycle phase, sampling ¹³C isotope patterns densely through time, and fitting a computational model of flux at each phase. The result transformed the picture: TCA cycle flux itself oscillates throughout the cell cycle, alternating between a glucose-driven mode (early cycle) and a glutamine-driven mode (later), with lactate and alanine exchange shifting in phase. The "static wheel" of the textbook is, in a living dividing cell, a periodically re-programming circuit — an example of systems biology discovering dynamics that steady-state biochemistry could never see.
v(t) = f(isotope labeling(t), metabolites(t)) — solved at each cell-cycle phase φTemporal fluxomics (Ahn & Antoniewicz 2017): instead of a single flux vector v, the model returns a time-resolved flux function, revealing periodic switching of the TCA cycle between glucose- and glutamine-oxidative modes.
Key papers
  • Ahn WK, Antoniewicz MR, et al. Temporal fluxomics reveals oscillations in TCA cycle flux throughout the mammalian cell cycle. Mol. Syst. Biol. 13, 953 (2017). — Molecular Systems Biology (free) · PubMed
62013 · Glycogen at the whole-body scale

Whole-Body Glycogen Models — Substrate Cycling Keeps Blood Glucose Alive

Old idea
Glycogen studies focused on a single tissue: the liver enzyme, the muscle enzyme, the isolated cascade. Blood glucose homeostasis was described verbally — insulin lowers, glucagon raises — without quantitative coupling.
The problem
Blood glucose is maintained by the interaction of liver, muscle, pancreas and blood — a multi-organ feedback loop that no single-enzyme study can capture. What design principle keeps it stable over hours of fasting? Why do liver and muscle both keep small opposing (futile) reactions running?
New model & finding
Whole-body computational models (Chalal & PLOS Comp Biol; also the composite "liver glucohomeostasis" models of Sedghizadeh & colleagues) integrate: glucagon/insulin receptor signaling → cAMP → Ca²⁺ → glycogen synthase / phosphorylase interconversion → glycogen storage → glucose-6-phosphate → blood glucose, with the pancreas closing the feedback loop. Simulation of the fed→fasted transition reproduced the physiological data and revealed a counterintuitive design principle: substrate cycling between glycogen synthesis and breakdown is not waste — it is critical for maintaining blood glucose homeostasis, because the mutual inhibition between phosphorylase-a and glycogen synthase phosphatase lets liver switch direction quickly while keeping the system stable. This is systems biology at the physiological scale: the model told researchers something the individual enzyme experiments had not.
dx/dt = f(glucose, insulin, glucagon, glycogen, enzyme states) — multi-compartment ODE model (liver, blood, pancreas, muscle)Whole-body glycogen regulation model (Chalal et al. 2013, PLOS Comput Biol): the liver glycogen control circuit is embedded in a closed glucose-insulin-glucagon feedback loop. Simulations of feeding→fasting transitions reproduce human data and expose the stabilizing role of substrate cycling.

Genome-scale metabolic models ( Recon, Ehcn ) embed the TCA cycle among thousands of reactions; machine-learning "kinetic learning" methods infer dynamic TCA parameters directly from ¹³C data — the two classic cycles are now cornerstones of computational systems biology.
