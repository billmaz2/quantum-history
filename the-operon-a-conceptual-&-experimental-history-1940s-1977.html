The Operon and the Promoter

How the idea that genes are switched on and off was invented in a Paris laboratory — and then, step by step, proved at the level of DNA, RNA, protein, and finally nucleotide sequence. An educational history from enzymatic adaptation to Maxam–Gilbert sequencing.

1 · Before the concept: "enzymatic adaptation" (1940s–1950s)

The story begins long before anyone knew what a gene was made of.

In the 1940s, Jacques Monod at the Pasteur Institute studied a puzzling phenomenon: when E. coli is fed lactose, it suddenly starts producing the enzyme β-galactosidase to digest it. Was the enzyme shaped by its substrate ("instruction"), or was it synthesized only when the substrate was present ("induction")? Using pulse-chase-style experiments with isotopic labeling, Monod and his colleagues showed that induction meant synthesis of new protein molecules, not reshaping old ones — and that the capacity to make the enzyme was inherited, even when the enzyme had never actually been made.

Meanwhile, geneticists in Monod's and François Jacob's circle collected mutants of lactose metabolism. Crucially, mutations in three adjacent activities — β-galactosidase (z), permease (y) and transacetylase (ac) — always behaved together in conjugation mapping: they were tightly clustered on the chromosome. Whatever "inducibility" was, it was a genetic, stable, heritable property.

Key conceptual hurdle: at this time many biologists believed ribosomes might be the genes themselves, and that regulation worked at the level of the cytoplasm. The operon model would break both assumptions at once.

2 · The model is born: operon, operator, regulator (1960–1961)

Two French notes and one enormous review article invented the vocabulary of gene regulation.

François Jacob arrived at the problem from phage λ, where he had shown that a bacterial virus can keep its genes silent for generations ("lysogeny"). The parallel with the silent lac genes struck him: in both cases, something represses gene expression, and the genes spring to life when repression is lifted. In 1960 the group published a short note to the French Academy of Sciences that named the new entity:

"L'opéron: groupe de gènes à expression coordonnée par un opérateur" — a group of genes whose expression is coordinated by an operator. — Jacob, Perrin, Sánchez & Monod, C. R. Acad. Sci. Paris 250:1727–1729 (1960)

The full theoretical synthesis came in 1961, in Jacob & Monod's 39-page review in the Journal of Molecular Biology — arguably the most influential paper in the history of molecular biology. It proposed:

  • The structural genes (z, y, ac) lie together and are transcribed as one unit — the operon.
  • Adjacent to them is a DNA segment called the operator, the target site at which expression is controlled.
  • A separate regulator gene (i) produces a diffusible repressor that binds the operator and blocks transcription of the whole operon.
  • An inducer (a β-galactoside such as lactose or IPTG) inactivates the repressor, releasing the genes.
  • Because transcription must be an unstable RNA copy of DNA, the paper also explicitly predicted the existence of messenger RNA before it was discovered.

The lac operon as Jacob & Monod conceived it in 1961. Note that every element — promoter, operator, structural genes, regulator gene — was at first only a genetic hypothesis. The rest of this page is the story of how each box was physically proved.

3 · How they proved the operon theory: the PaJaMa experiment (1957–1959)

The logic of the model was tested with bacterial sex and clever genetics — before anyone knew what the repressor was made of.

Two mutant classes existed: lacI⁻ mutants (constitutive — they make β-galactosidase all the time, with or without inducer) and lacZ⁻ mutants (unable to make the enzyme). Two rival hypotheses explained constitutivity:

  • Monod's "internal inducer" idea: lacI⁻ cells produce their own inducer → the inducer is a positive activator, and constitutivity should be dominant.
  • Jacob's repressor idea: lacI⁺ makes a diffusible repressor; lacI⁻ makes none → the inducer merely neutralizes repressor, and lacI⁻ should be recessive.
The experiment Arthur Pardee (on sabbatical from Berkeley), Jacob and Monod — "PaJaMa" — constructed partial diploids (merozygotes): by conjugation, a donor Hfr ♂ cell carrying one lac allele injects its DNA into a recipient ♀ F⁻ cell carrying a different allele, creating a cell with two copies of the lac region for a few hours. They then measured whether β-galactosidase synthesis was induced or constitutive in these transient diploids, with and without the inducer.

4 · Proving the pieces were DNA and protein — not RNA

By 1961 the model was genetic. The next decade answered three physical questions: What is the operator made of? What is the promoter made of? What is the repressor made of?

4.1 · Why the operator and promoter had to be DNA

The decisive argument was the genetics itself, combined with mapping:

  • Operator = DNA. Operator mutations (lacOᶜ) behaved as point mutations on the chromosome: they mapped by recombination to a tiny segment between lacI and lacZ, they were inherited with the DNA through conjugation, and their effect was strictly cis-limited — only RNA-linked to genes on the very DNA molecule carrying the mutation. A diffusible RNA or protein could never be cis-limited; only a site on DNA can act only on genes physically attached to it. Deletions of the region behaved exactly as DNA-deletion genetics predicted.
  • Promoter = DNA. Jacob, Ullman & Monod (1964) defined the promoter ("le promoteur") genetically: promoter mutations (lacp⁻) abolish expression of all genes of the operon in cis, but cannot be complemented by a good promoter on another chromosome — again the signature of a site, not a product. Like the operator, promoter mutations mapped, recombined, and were transduced like DNA markers. The promoter was proposed as the site where RNA polymerase initiates transcription of the operon — the "genetic element necessary for the expression of an operon."

Reference: Jacob, Ullman & Monod, C. R. Acad. Sci. Paris 258:3125–3128 (1964) — "Le promoteur, élément génétique nécessaire à l'expression d'un opéron." (Comptes Rendus of 1964 have no DOI; the citation is verifiable via PubMed/Crossref record of the 1965 English account, J. Mol. Biol. 12 (1965).)

4.2 · Why the regulator product had to be a protein, not RNA

Jacob and Monod initially hedged: the repressor could be DNA, RNA, or protein. Several results forced the protein answer:

  • Complementation tests in diploids showed lacI acts trans, so it makes a diffusible gene product — but the product had to be specific and inducibly-bindable to β-galactosides.
  • Inducer specificity. Repression is lifted by small β-galactoside molecules (IPTG, ONPF). A regulatory RNA would be made from a DNA template and could not "recognize" an arbitrary small molecule shaped like galactose — but proteins, as Monod & Changeux were arguing in their allostery theory, bind small effectors with exquisite specificity. Allostery made "repressor = protein" chemically plausible.
  • The I⁻ᵈ / Iˢ genetics: non-inducible ("super-repressed") lacIˢ mutants and temperature-sensitive lacI mutants behaved like missense mutations in a protein-coding gene, and nonsense lacI mutations could be suppressed by tRNA suppressors (Müller-Hill 1966) — a suppressible amber phenotype is the classic proof that a gene acts through a translated polypeptide.

Allostery reference: Monod, Changeux & Jacob, J. Mol. Biol. 6:306–329 (1963) doi:10.1016/S0022-2836(63)80091-1. Suppressible lacI mutants: Müller-Hill, J. Mol. Biol. 15:374–376 (1966) doi:10.1016/S0022-2836(66)80234-6.

✅ Verdict: operator and promoter = cis-acting DNA sites; regulator gene = trans-acting gene encoding a protein. But in 1961–65 this was still inference. The physical proof — actually isolating the protein and showing it binds DNA — took until 1966–67 (Section 6).

5 · Brenner, Jacob & Meselson: proving messenger RNA (1961)

The operon model required a short-lived RNA copy of DNA — and in one weekend experiment, it was found.

The operon model's repressor was proposed to block transcription — the synthesis of an RNA copy of the genes. But this clashed with the dominant view of the day: ribosomes themselves were thought to be the gene products, each ribosome templating its own protein. Jacob & Monod's 1961 paper explicitly reasoned that no, genes must send out an unstable intermediate — a "messenger" — and even named the concept before it was demonstrated.

The Caltech experiment, spring 1961 Sydney Brenner and François Jacob (visiting Caltech) joined Matthew Meselson. The plan, formulated over milkshakes in Pasadena: if ribosomes are permanent gene-specific templates, a bacterium infected with phage T4 should keep making old ribosomes but produce T4 proteins on new ribosomes carrying T4-specific RNA. If instead ribosomes are inert machinery and only the RNA changes, phage proteins should appear on the same old ribosomes, now loaded with a fresh, unstable RNA.
  1. Infect E. coli with T4 in heavy medium (¹³C, ¹⁵N) so all ribosomes made before infection are "light" — wait: cells are first grown in heavy isotope medium so pre-infection ribosomes are heavy, then infected and shifted to light medium, so any new ribosomes would be light.
  2. Pulse-label newly synthesized RNA with ³²P after infection.
  3. Separate heavy (old) vs. light (new) ribosomes by density-gradient centrifugation in the Meselson–Stahl analytical ultracentrifuge.
Result: phage protein synthesis ran on old, heavy ribosomes; the ³²P-labeled RNA formed a rapidly turning-over, phage-specific RNA bound to those old ribosomes. Ribosomes were generic machines; the message was a separate, unstable RNA — messenger RNA.

Simultaneously at Harvard, Gros, Hiatt, Gilbert, Kurland, Risebrough & Watson caught the same unstable RNA in uninfected E. coli by pulse labeling — the two papers were published back-to-back in Nature.

✅ Verdict: mRNA was real: an unstable RNA intermediate carrying information from genes (DNA) to ribosomes. This completed the operon mechanism — repressors control whether the mRNA is made at all, and the mRNA is the short-lived intermediary. It also collapsed the "ribosome = gene" theory forever.

Primary papers: Brenner, Jacob & Meselson, Nature 190:576–581 (1961) doi:10.1038/190576a0; Gros, Hiatt, Gilbert, Kurland, Risebrough & Watson, Nature 190:581–585 (1961) doi:10.1038/190581a0.

6 · Isolating the repressors: Gilbert, Müller-Hill and Ptashne (1966–1967)

For five years the repressor was a hypothetical protein that most of the field doubted could ever be caught. Two young scientists caught it — in two different systems — within months of each other.

The problem was brutal: a lac repressor molecule exists at only ~10 copies per E. coli cell (≈0.001% of cell protein). Assays of sufficient sensitivity had to be invented first.

6.1 · The lac repressor (Gilbert & Müller-Hill, 1966)

Strategy Walter Gilbert and Benno Müller-Hill, at Harvard, used a trick of genetics plus a trick of chemistry: (1) they built a lacI⁺ / F-lacI⁺ diploid strain overproducing the repressor, and (2) they screened for repressor activity by equilibrium dialysis — measuring binding of radioactive ¹⁴C-IPTG (the gratuitous inducer) to protein fractions. Fractions from lacI⁺ cells, but not lacI⁻ cells, bound the galactoside specifically. The binding activity co-purified with a ~150-residue protein that behaved exactly as the lacI product: inducer binding followed by release of the operator.

Paper: Gilbert & Müller-Hill, Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. USA 56:1891–1898 (1966) doi:10.1073/pnas.56.6.1891. A year later Gilbert showed the repressor binds operator DNA specifically, physically confirming the 1961 model.

6.2 · The λ repressor (Ptashne, 1967)

Strategy Mark Ptashne, also at Harvard (in a lab next door, in open rivalry with Gilbert), hunted the repressor of phage λ — the protein Jacob had imagined keeping lysogenic phage genes silent for generations. Ptashne used a mutant λ prophage carrying the λcI temperature-sensitive gene, overexpressed the cI product, and assayed fractions for binding to radioactively labeled λ DNA (and, critically, to DNA from a mutant phage with a defective operator — which bound less well, proving sequence-specific recognition). The isolated λ repressor proved to be a protein of ~27 kDa that bound λ operator DNA with high affinity and specificity, exactly as the operon model demanded.

Papers: Ptashne, Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. USA 57:306–313 (1967) doi:10.1073/pnas.57.2.306; Ptashne, Nature 214:232–234 (1967) doi:10.1038/214232a0 (specific binding of the isolated repressor to λ DNA).

Ptashne went on to dissect the λ switch in fine chemical detail (operators OR1/OR2/OR3, cooperative binding, the autoregulatory circuit), summarized in his classic monograph A Genetic Switch (1986), and Maniatis & Ptashne determined the structure of the λ operators in 1973 (Nature 246:133–136 doi:10.1038/246133a0).

✅ Verdict: the repressor was finally a molecule in a test tube: a protein, made by a regulator gene, that recognizes a specific DNA sequence — the operator. The last purely speculative element of Jacob and Monod's model became physical biochemistry.

7 · Gilbert and the promoter: reading the DNA sequence itself (1973–1977)

Once the proteins were in hand, Gilbert used them as molecular tools to find and sequence the control region — and in doing so invented DNA sequencing.

7.1 · Using the repressor to purify and sequence the operator (1973)

The "protection" trick Gilbert & Maxam reasoned: the repressor binds the operator and nothing else. So they let purified lac repressor bind naked lac-region DNA, then added DNase. The DNA covered by repressor was protected from digestion; everything else was chopped away. The protected ~27-base-pair fragment was then recovered, transcribed in vitro, and sequenced by the RNA methods of the day (Sanger's RNA fingerprinting / copying into RNA and sequencing that). This was the first gene-control DNA element ever sequenced.

Paper: Gilbert & Maxam, Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. USA 70:3581–3584 (1973) doi:10.1073/pnas.70.12.3581 — "The nucleotide sequence of the lac operator." In the same issue, Maizels & Gilbert sequenced the 5′ end of lac mRNA made from the strong UV5 promoter mutant, locating the transcription start point and hence the promoter on the DNA map (PNAS 70:3585–3589 doi:10.1073/pnas.70.12.3585).

7.2 · Sequencing the promoter (1975)

With the operator sequence and the mRNA start site known, the neighboring promoter was pinned down. Dickson, Abelson, Barnes & Reznikoff compiled the full sequence of the lac promoter–operator region and showed it contained the information for binding the two proteins that control lac transcription: RNA polymerase and the CAP–cAMP positive activator (Science 187:27–35 doi:10.1126/science.1088926). Majors sequenced the promoter of the lacI gene itself and its Iᵍ "up-promoter" mutation, a single base change at position −35 increasing promoter strength tenfold (PNAS 72:4394–4398 doi:10.1073/pnas.72.11.4394) — the beginning of the now-standard picture of the −35 and −10 promoter elements.

7.3 · Maxam–Gilbert chemical sequencing (1977)

The RNA-copying tricks used in 1973 were laborious and indirect. Gilbert and Allan Maxam replaced them with a general method: label the DNA at one end, cleave it chemically and partially at each of the four bases with base-specific reagents (hydrazine, dimethyl sulfate, formic acid, and acid), then read the ladder of fragments on a polyacrylamide gel. Any DNA could now be sequenced directly — no cloning, no copying into RNA.

✅ Verdict: Gilbert's hunt for the promoter's sequence produced the first DNA sequencing method. Maxam & Gilbert, Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. USA 74:560–564 (1977) doi:10.1073/pnas.74.2.560. Sanger's chain-termination method appeared the same year; Gilbert and Sanger shared the 1980 Nobel Prize in Chemistry (with Paul Berg). The road from a 1961 genetic hypothesis to reading the actual control sequences, base by base, was complete — and it opened the way to sequencing every gene, every operon, and eventually whole genomes.
