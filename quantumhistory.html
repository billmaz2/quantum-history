From Bohr to Feynman: Quantum Physics 1900–1950

How the Bohr model became the Dirac equation, quantum field theory, and Feynman diagrams — with the actual equations, how they were used, and what they revealed
Abstract. This survey traces the conceptual and mathematical arc from Bohr's quantized orbits (1913) to Feynman's diagrams (1949). For each era, we present the central equation, explain how physicists used it, and state what it revealed. Special attention is given to second quantization — how Dirac, Heisenberg, and Pauli turned the wavefunction itself into an operator, making the creation and annihilation of particles possible, and thereby founding quantum field theory.

1. Pre-Quantum Foundations and the Bohr Model (1900–1913)

Old quantum theory Quantization

Planck (1900) — The quantum of action

Equation: E = hν Planck proposed that energy exchange between matter and radiation occurs in discrete packets (quanta) proportional to frequency. h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s.

How it was used: Planck derived the blackbody radiation spectrum by counting oscillators with discrete energies En = nhν and using Boltzmann statistics. The result fit the experimental curve perfectly at all frequencies, eliminating the ultraviolet catastrophe predicted by classical equipartition.

What it revealed: Energy is not continuous. This was the first crack in classical physics. Planck himself thought it was a mathematical trick; Einstein took it literally. DOI: 10.1002/andp.19013090310

Einstein (1905) — The photon

Equation: Ekinetic = hν − W where W is the work function of the metal.

How it was used: Einstein proposed that light itself consists of quanta (photons). The photoelectric effect was explained by treating light as a stream of particles each carrying energy hν. An electron absorbs one photon and escapes if hν > W.

What it revealed: Quantization is not just in matter — the electromagnetic field itself is quantized. This was a radical step toward quantum field theory. DOI: 10.1002/andp.19053220607

Rutherford (1911) — The nuclear atom

Result: Alpha particles scattered off a thin gold foil showed large-angle deflections, implying a tiny, massive, positively charged nucleus. The atom is mostly empty space.

Problem: Classical electrodynamics says an accelerating electron radiates energy. An electron orbiting a nucleus should spiral inward in ~10⁻¹¹ seconds. The atom is unstable. Classical physics fails. DOI: 10.1080/14786440508637080

Bohr (1913) — Quantized orbits

Equations: L = nħ   (quantized angular momentum) En = − (mee⁴) / (8ε₀²h²n²)   (hydrogen energy levels) hν = En₂ − En₁   (Bohr frequency condition)

How it was used: Bohr postulated that electrons occupy discrete stationary states with quantized angular momentum L = nħ. Radiation is emitted or absorbed only when the electron jumps between states, with photon energy equal to the energy difference. For hydrogen, this gave:

What it revealed: The Rydberg constant R∞ could be calculated from fundamental constants (me, e, h, ε₀) and matched experiment to within 0.05%. This was a stunning triumph. But the model could not explain multi-electron atoms, spectral intensities, or the Zeeman effect. It was a hybrid of classical orbits and quantum rules. DOI: 10.1080/14786441308634955 CERN Record

Why Bohr's model had to be replaced: The model worked only for hydrogen. It could not handle helium. It assumed electrons have well-defined orbits, contradicting the uncertainty principle that would come later. It was a patchwork — and it was clear that a deeper theory was needed.

2. The Old Quantum Theory and Its Breakdown (1913–1925)

Correspondence principle Sommerfeld

Sommerfeld (1916) — Elliptical orbits and fine structure

Equation: α = e² / (4πε₀ħc) ≈ 1/137   (fine-structure constant)

How it was used: Sommerfeld extended Bohr's model with elliptical orbits and relativistic corrections. He introduced a second quantum number l (azimuthal) and a magnetic quantum number m. The relativistic correction split the hydrogen energy levels into fine structure.

What it revealed: The fine-structure constant α emerged as a fundamental dimensionless parameter governing the strength of electromagnetic interactions. But the model still could not handle multi-electron atoms. DOI: 10.1002/andp.19163561702

Bohr (1918–1923) — The correspondence principle

Statement: In the limit of large quantum numbers (n → ∞), quantum predictions must converge to classical ones.

How it was used: Bohr used this as a guiding principle to construct quantum rules. For example, the frequency of radiation from a transition n → n−1 for large n should equal the classical orbital frequency. This helped determine selection rules and intensities.

What it revealed: Quantum mechanics must contain classical mechanics as a limiting case — a crucial consistency requirement that any new theory must satisfy. DOI: 10.1007/BF01328531

Kramers & Heisenberg (1924) — Virtual oscillators

Idea: Replace classical orbital frequencies with "virtual oscillator" frequencies corresponding to all possible transition pairs (n, m). Each pair has frequency νnm = (En − Em)/h and an amplitude that becomes the matrix element in Heisenberg's theory.

What it revealed: The intensity of spectral lines is determined by these transition amplitudes. This was the direct precursor to matrix mechanics. Heisenberg would soon realize that these amplitudes form matrices. DOI: 10.1007/BF01328280

3. The New Quantum Mechanics (1925–1927)

Matrix mechanics Wave mechanics Uncertainty

Heisenberg (1925) — Matrix mechanics

Equations: x(t) → xmn eiωmnt   (observable transition amplitudes) x p − p x = iħ I   (canonical commutation relation)

How it was used: Heisenberg replaced classical position x(t) with a set of transition amplitudes xmn indexed by pairs of stationary states. The multiplication rule for these amplitudes is non-commutative: (x p)mn = Σk xmk pkn Born and Jordan recognized this as matrix multiplication.

What it revealed: Physical observables are non-commuting operators. The uncertainty principle and the entire structure of quantum mechanics follow from this. Orbits are gone — only transition amplitudes remain. DOI: 10.1007/BF01328377 Born–Jordan DOI: 10.1007/BF01328280

Schrödinger (1926) — Wave mechanics

Equation: iħ ∂ψ/∂t = Ĥψ = [ −(ħ²/2m)∇² + V(r) ] ψ

How it was used: Schrödinger replaced the discrete orbit with a continuous wavefunction ψ(r,t). For the hydrogen atom, solving this equation gives quantized energy levels En and wavefunctions ψnlm(r,θ,φ) — exactly reproducing Bohr's energy levels but with a proper mathematical foundation.

What it revealed: The wavefunction describes the quantum state. Its absolute square |ψ|² gives the probability density. The discrete energy levels emerge naturally from boundary conditions (the wavefunction must be normalizable), not from ad hoc postulates. DOI: 10.1002/andp.19263840404 Part II DOI: 10.1002/andp.19263840602

Born (1926) — Probabilistic interpretation

Equation: P(r) d³r = |ψ(r)|² d³r

How it was used: Born interpreted |ψ|² as the probability density of finding the particle at position r. This gave physical meaning to the wavefunction and resolved the wave-particle duality.

What it revealed: Quantum mechanics is fundamentally probabilistic. Determinism is gone. This became a cornerstone of the Copenhagen interpretation. DOI: 10.1007/BF01397477

Heisenberg (1927) — Uncertainty principle

Equation: Δx · Δp ≥ ħ/2

How it was used: This relation limits the precision with which complementary observables (position and momentum) can be simultaneously known. It follows directly from the commutation relation [x, p] = iħ.

What it revealed: The classical concept of a trajectory (a path with definite position and momentum at each moment) is fundamentally incompatible with quantum mechanics. Bohr's orbits are meaningless. DOI: 10.1007/BF01397280

Bohr (1928) — Complementarity

Statement: Quantum objects exhibit wave and particle properties that are mutually exclusive but complementary. The full description requires both, but they cannot be observed simultaneously.

What it revealed: This became the philosophical foundation of the Copenhagen interpretation. It resolved paradoxes like the double-slit experiment by asserting that the experimental arrangement determines which aspect (wave or particle) is manifest. DOI: 10.1038/121580a0

The break from Bohr: The new quantum mechanics abolished orbits. It described states, wavefunctions, and probabilities. The Bohr model was superseded within 13 years. But its central concept — stationary states with discrete energies — survived intact as the eigenvalues of the Hamiltonian.

4. Quantum Field Theory Is Born (1927–1928)

Field quantization Relativistic electron Second quantization

Dirac (1927) — Quantizing the electromagnetic field

Equation (field expansion): A(r,t) = Σk,λ √(ħ / 2ε₀ωkV) [ akλ εkλ ei(k·r − ωkt) + a†kλ ε*kλ e−i(k·r − ωkt) ]

How it was used: Dirac treated the electromagnetic field as a collection of harmonic oscillators — one for each mode (wavevector k and polarization λ). He then quantized each oscillator using the standard rules: [akλ, a†k'λ'] = δkk' δλλ'

The operator a†kλ creates a photon in mode (k,λ); akλ annihilates one. The Hamiltonian becomes: H = Σk,λ ħωk (a†kλakλ + ½)

What it revealed: Photons are quantized excitations of the electromagnetic field. The field itself is a quantum object. This was the first complete quantum field theory. It explained spontaneous emission, the Lamb shift (later), and the anomalous magnetic moment of the electron. DOI: 10.1098/rspa.1927.0039

Dirac (1928) — The relativistic electron

Equation (Dirac equation): (iħγμ∂μ − mc) ψ = 0 where γμ are 4×4 gamma matrices satisfying {γμ, γν} = 2gμν.

How it was used: Dirac sought a relativistic wave equation linear in both space and time derivatives. The gamma matrices were forced by the requirement that the equation be consistent with the relativistic energy-momentum relation E² = p²c² + m²c⁴. The solution ψ is a four-component spinor — two components for spin up/down, two for particle/antiparticle.

What it revealed: The electron has spin ½ naturally — it is not put in by hand. The equation predicts negative-energy solutions, which Dirac interpreted as a "sea" of filled states. A hole in this sea is a positron (antimatter), discovered by Anderson in 1932. The Dirac equation also predicted the electron's magnetic moment g = 2, which was later refined by QED to g = 2.002319... DOI: 10.1098/rspa.1928.0023

Jordan & Wigner (1928) — Second quantization of matter

Key idea: Just as the electromagnetic field can be quantized (photons), the Schrödinger wavefunction ψ can itself be promoted to an operator that creates and annihilates particles.

Equation (field operator): ψ(r) = Σk ak φk(r) [ak, a†k']∓ = δkk' where the commutator (−) applies to bosons and the anticommutator (+) to fermions.

How it was used: The many-body wavefunction is replaced by a field operator ψ(r) that acts on a Fock space of occupation numbers. The operator a†k creates a particle in state k; ak annihilates one. The Hamiltonian becomes: H = Σk Ek a†kak + ½ Σk,k',q V(q) a†k+q a†k'−q ak' ak

What it revealed: Particles can be created and destroyed. The number of particles is not fixed. This is the essence of quantum field theory. For fermions, the anticommutation relations automatically enforce the Pauli exclusion principle — two fermions cannot occupy the same state because (a†k)² = 0. DOI: 10.1007/BF01336751 Part II DOI: 10.1007/BF01340265

How second quantization was possible: The crucial insight was that the wavefunction ψ is not a probability amplitude but a field operator. In first quantization (Schrödinger), ψ is a complex number at each point in space. In second quantization, ψ(r) becomes an operator that acts on a Hilbert space of variable particle number. The canonical commutation relations for a and a† replace the classical Poisson brackets. This works because the harmonic oscillator spectrum is isomorphic to the spectrum of occupation numbers. The same mathematical structure describes photons (bosons) and electrons (fermions) — only the commutation relations differ (commutator vs. anticommutator). This unified matter and radiation as quantum fields.

5. The QED Crisis and the Road to Renormalization (1930–1948)

Infinities Infrared divergence Shelter Island

Mott (1929, 1931) — Infrared divergence

Calculation: Mott calculated relativistic electron scattering in a Coulomb potential using the Dirac equation. He found that the probability of emitting arbitrarily low-energy photons diverges: P ∝ ∫ dω/ω → ∞   (as ω → 0)

What it revealed: The divergence arises because a charged particle can never be truly free — it always emits and reabsorbs soft photons. The resolution (later) was that the observed electron is always accompanied by a cloud of soft photons, and the divergence cancels between emission and virtual corrections. DOI: 10.1098/rspa.1929.0186

Dancoff (1939) — The missed opportunity

Calculation: Dancoff attempted to calculate radiative corrections to electron scattering using second-order perturbation theory. He encountered divergent integrals but made a sign error that prevented him from seeing that the divergence could be absorbed into a redefinition of the electron charge and mass.

What it revealed: The machinery for renormalization was almost there. The sign error delayed progress by nearly a decade. DOI: 10.1103/PhysRev.55.959

Shelter Island Conference (1947) — The crisis crystallizes

Key experimental results reported:

  • Lamb shift: The 2S1/2 and 2P1/2 levels of hydrogen are not degenerate; there is a small splitting of ~1057 MHz. Dirac's equation predicts they should be exactly degenerate.
  • Anomalous magnetic moment: The electron's magnetic moment is slightly larger than the Dirac value g = 2. The excess is (g−2)/2 ≈ 0.00118.

What it revealed: QED predictions disagreed with experiment. The infinities in the theory were not just a mathematical nuisance — they had physical consequences. A new formulation was needed. CERN History Record

Bethe (1947) — The first renormalization

Calculation: Bethe performed a non-relativistic calculation of the Lamb shift. He found that the divergent integral for the electron self-energy could be absorbed into a redefinition of the electron mass: mobserved = mbare + δm δm = (3α/2π) m ln(ħ/Δx) → ∞

How it was used: Bethe replaced the divergent logarithm with a cutoff at the electron Compton wavelength. The finite remainder gave a Lamb shift of ~1040 MHz — close to the experimental 1057 MHz.

What it revealed: Infinities can be absorbed into unobservable "bare" parameters. The physical predictions are finite. This was the first practical demonstration of renormalization. DOI: 10.1103/PhysRev.72.339

Schwinger, Feynman, Tomonaga (1948–1949) — Three independent formulations

Schwinger (1948): Developed a covariant field theory with a unitary transformation that separated finite from divergent parts. He calculated the electron's anomalous magnetic moment to order α: (g−2)/2 = α/(2π) ≈ 0.001161 This matched experiment. DOI: 10.1103/PhysRev.74.1439

Tomonaga (1946): Developed super-many-time theory, a covariant generalization of the interaction picture. Independently arrived at the same renormalization program. DOI: 10.1143/PTP.1.27

Feynman (1949): Developed the path integral formulation and introduced Feynman diagrams — a graphical representation of perturbative QED. Each diagram corresponds to a mathematical expression: Amplitude = ∫ [d³p/(2π)³] Πvertices (coupling) Πpropagators (1/(p² − m²)) The diagrams made it possible to organize the calculation of physical processes systematically. DOI: 10.1103/PhysRev.76.769 DOI: 10.1103/PhysRev.76.749

Dyson (1949) — Equivalence and renormalizability

Key results:

  • Proved that the Schwinger, Feynman, and Tomonaga formulations are mathematically equivalent.
  • Demonstrated that QED is renormalizable to all orders in perturbation theory.
  • Showed that only a finite number of divergent constants (mass, charge, wavefunction renormalization) are needed.

What it revealed: QED is a consistent quantum field theory. The infinities are absorbed into unobservable bare parameters. Physical predictions are finite and match experiment to extraordinary precision. DOI: 10.1103/PhysRev.75.486

6. How Feynman Diagrams Work

Perturbation theory Graphical calculus

A Feynman diagram is a pictorial representation of a term in the perturbative expansion of a quantum field theory. Each element has a precise mathematical meaning:

Diagram Element Mathematical Factor Physical Meaning
Solid line (electron) i / (p̸ − m + iε) Electron propagator
Wavy line (photon) −i gμν / (k² + iε) Photon propagator
Vertex −i e γμ Electron-photon interaction
Loop ∫ d⁴k / (2π)⁴ Virtual particle momentum integral

Example — electron scattering (Møller scattering): The lowest-order diagram has two electrons exchanging a virtual photon. The amplitude is: M = (e² / q²) [ū(p₃)γμu(p₁)] [ū(p₄)γμu(p₂)] where q = p₁ − p₃ is the momentum transfer. Squaring this amplitude and integrating over phase space gives the cross-section, which agrees with experiment.

What diagrams revealed: They provide an intuitive, systematic way to compute physical quantities. They make divergences manifest (loops → divergent integrals) and show how renormalization works (divergences absorbed into vertex and propagator corrections). They have become the universal language of quantum field theory, extending to QCD, electroweak theory, and beyond.

7. Summary: The Conceptual Arc

Era Central Equation What It Revealed
1900–1913 E = hν; L = nħ Energy and angular momentum are quantized; atom is stable
1925–1927 [x,p] = iħ; iħ∂ψ/∂t = Ĥψ Observables are operators; |ψ|² is probability; uncertainty
1927–1930 (iħγμ∂μ − mc)ψ = 0; [a,a†] = 1 Electron is relativistic spinor; particles are field quanta
1930–1948 ∫ dω/ω → ∞; mobs = mbare + δm QED predicts infinities; renormalization absorbs them
1948–1949 Amplitude = Σ diagrams Feynman diagrams systematize calculation; QED is renormalizable

Core logic: The Bohr model quantized energy but kept classical orbits. Heisenberg and Schrödinger abolished orbits and introduced operators and wavefunctions. Dirac made the electron relativistic and predicted antimatter. Second quantization turned the wavefunction into a field operator, making particle creation and annihilation possible. Quantum field theory quantized the electromagnetic field, creating infinities. Feynman diagrams and renormalization tamed those infinities, turning QED into the most precisely tested theory in physics. Each step was forced by the failure of the previous framework to explain new data or internal consistency.
